## Read

The bear case is a credit-deterioration scenario. The Ameris portfolio contains loan quality that hasn't been stress-tested through a full rate cycle or economic downturn. If NPL ratios spike above 1.0% — which is typical in the 18-24 months post-merger for regional banks where underwriting standards converge — the provision for credit losses will consume 30-40% of pretax income, ROE will drop below 8%, and the P/B will compress from 1.23x toward 0.90x. The 10-year CAGR of -15.4% is the worst outcome and reflects the value trap risk: a bank that looks cheap at 1.23x P/B but whose earnings are structurally declining due to credit provisioning.

The base case is the most likely outcome. Revenue stabilizes around 5% annual growth — consistent with analyst consensus — as the merged franchise achieves operating synergies and the Midwest deposit base continues to generate stable, low-cost funding. The key number is the 6% BVPS CAGR: at $20.83 common BVPS with a 10% ROE and a 29% dividend payout, retained earnings of ~71% of EPS of ~$2.80 produces approximately 6% annual BVPS growth, which compounds steadily. The 10-year price CAGR of +20.8% reflects book value compounding plus modest P/B stability.

The bull case requires ROE to improve from the current 10.08% toward 13-14% — which means the Ameris synergies produce better-than-expected cost savings, the deposit franchise holds pricing power, and management returns capital via aggressive buybacks. This is achievable but requires all inputs to be favorable simultaneously. The P/B expansion to 1.50x is within historical precedent (WSBC at 1.8x, THFF at 1.7x), but requires sustained above-average ROE that hasn't been demonstrated yet.

Stock prices in each case work backward from explicit year-10 terminal P/B anchors (0.90x / 1.20x / 1.50x), consistent with the workbook model's P/B + ROE framework. The P/E columns are cross-checks that reflect the fact that ONB is currently trading at moderate P/E multiples (10.1x forward, 13.6x trailing) relative to its earnings — as book value compounds, these multiples will naturally adjust unless earnings per share grow faster than price.
