## 1. Thesis In One Page

### The Setup

Old National Bancorp looks like a regional bank that has successfully executed a transformative acquisition — the Ameris Bancorp deal that closed in mid-2024 — and is now seeing the financial benefits flow through the income statement. Revenue jumped from $1.89 billion in FY2024 to $2.52 billion in FY2025, a 34% increase. Net interest income grew from $1.53 billion to $2.06 billion in the same window. The stock is trading at 1.23x book value and 10.1x forward earnings — numbers that look attractive for a financial services company.

But the real question is whether this post-acquisition scale has produced durable franchise economics, or whether the balance sheet expansion hides credit-quality and capital-adequacy risks that typically accompany rapid regional-bank growth. The Ameris acquisition expanded total assets from $53.6 billion to $72.2 billion in one year — a 35% jump — and share count grew 24% from stock consideration. The company is now roughly 25% larger with a different geographic profile (adding Georgia and Carolinas exposure to a traditional Indiana/Michigan/Ohio/Tennessee/Arkansas footprint).

The thesis works if the Ameris integration is proceeding on plan, deposits migrate efficiently, loan quality holds through the rate-cycle downturn, and management maintains ROE above the ~9-10% cost of equity while returning capital via the dividend. It fails if NPL ratios tick sharply higher in the merged portfolio, if the cost of deposits accelerates faster than asset repricing, or if the CET1 ratio proves inadequate for the combined balance sheet and forces dilutive recapitalization.

### The Core Debate

The debate is not whether Old National is growing. The acquisition-driven revenue and asset expansion is already booked. The debate is whether the growth is coming from stable deposit-funded lending in profitable geographies, or whether the Ameris portfolio contains hidden credit risk, higher-cost deposit funding, or CRE concentrations that could erode earnings once rate-cycle conditions normalize.

If the former is true — if the merged franchise compounding book value at 8-10% ROE with manageable capital ratios — then the current 1.23x P/B is a discount to what peers like WSBC (1.8-2.0x P/B), THFF (1.5-1.7x P/B), and BUSE (1.3x P/B) command. If the latter is true — if the combined loan portfolio has deteriorating credit metrics or the deposit franchise is less sticky than the Indiana/Ohio base — the current P/B already reflects that risk.

## 2. Company Story

### Origin And Evolution

Old National Bancorp traces its roots to 1834, making it one of the older continuously operating bank holding companies in the United States. Headquartered in Evansville, Indiana, the bank grew organically through the Midwest — establishing a core franchise across Indiana, Ohio, Michigan, Tennessee, and Arkansas. For most of its modern history, Old National was a classic "branch-by-branch" regional bank that prioritized market penetration and deposit gathering over aggressive merger activity.

The strategic inflection point arrived in 2024 with the acquisition of Ameris Bancorp, a Georgia-headquartered bank with operations extending across the Southeast. The deal expanded ONB's asset base by roughly $18.6 billion (from $53.6B to $72.2B) and added Georgia, South Carolina, and North Carolina to its footprint. This was a deliberate geographic diversification play: moving from a Midwest-heavy concentration into high-growth Southern markets.

The acquisition also fundamentally changed the company's scale profile. With $72.2 billion in total assets and $10.19 billion in market capitalization, ONB moved from a "micro- to small-cap regional" into the mid-tier of the regional banking universe. Share count expanded from 311 million to 386 million through stock consideration, and total debt rose from $5.14 billion to approximately $8.3 billion.

### Why The Company Matters

Old National matters because it represents the typical mid-cap regional bank's response to the consolidation cycle and the post-2023 banking sector stress. After the collapses of Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, and First Republic in 2023 — and the subsequent stress tests of Credit Suisse and other intermediaries — the FDIC and OCC tightened scrutiny on regional bank balance sheets. Smaller banks either consolidated into larger peers or needed to demonstrate superior capital positions.

Old National chose the consolidation path. The Ameris acquisition was strategic: it added $18.6B in assets, diversified the geographic risk profile, and created cross-selling opportunities. The question is whether the acquisition priced well, whether the combined entity's credit quality is sound, and whether management can return the ROE to above the cost of equity.

## 3. Business Model

### How The Company Makes Money

Old National operates as a traditional bank holding company, with Old National Bank as the operating subsidiary. Revenue comes from three primary channels:

Net interest income is the dominant revenue driver. ONB accepts deposits — including noninterest-bearing demand accounts, interest-bearing checking, savings, money market, and time deposits — and deploys that capital into loans. The loan portfolio comprises residential real estate, commercial real estate, commercial loans, agricultural loans, consumer loans, and home equity lines of credit. The net interest margin (NIM) on this spread represents the bulk of earnings. For FY2025, NII was $2.06 billion on $2.52 billion total revenue — roughly 82% of the income pie.

Noninterest income provides the second revenue stream. This includes service charges on deposit accounts, debit/ATM card fees, trust and wealth management fees, brokerage commissions, foreign currency services, and treasury management and merchant services for commercial clients. On TTM data, operating margin ran at 51.26%, which is high for a bank because noninterest income carries higher margins than the interest spread business.

The company also offers capital markets services and community development lending/equity investment solutions, which provides ancillary revenue in the commercial and treasury management segment.

### The Economic Engine

The engine is: acquire deposits at competitive rates, underwrite credit selectively across commercial and consumer loans and real estate, manage the interest-rate cycle to maintain a positive duration gap, compound book value through retained earnings, and return surplus capital via dividends and selective share buybacks. The franchise's competitive advantage is its Midwest market penetration (Indiana, Ohio, Michigan) combined with the newly added Southeast footprint (Georgia, Carolinas) from the Ameris acquisition.

The weak link is deposit pricing discipline in a rising-rate environment. When short-term rates rise, banks face a choice: absorb the higher funding cost (compressing NIM) or reprice deposits passively, risking flight to money market funds or larger competitors. Old National's deposit beta — the rate at which its deposit costs follow market rates — is the critical near-term variable. A deposit beta above 60-70% would compress NIM, while a well-managed beta below 50% preserves the interest spread.

Additionally, the integration of the Ameris portfolio introduces an execution risk that doesn't exist in organic growth. The $18.6 billion in acquired assets include loan portfolios that may have different underwriting standards, different CRE concentrations, and different customer migration patterns. The first two years of combined operations are the test period.

### Unit Economics

For a bank, the unit economics are fundamentally different from a product company. The analogue to "customer acquisition cost" is the cost of deposits (rate paid on transaction deposits and CDs). The analogue to "retention" is deposit turnover. The analogue to "revenue per customer" is the revenue per deposit dollar.

Old National's cost of deposits for FY2025 was approximately 1.70% (interest expense of $1.21B / average deposits estimate). Yield on earning assets was approximately 4.30% (interest income of $3.27B / average earning assets). The NIM of approximately 2.60% is healthy for a regional bank and compares favorably to the sector average of approximately 2.40-2.50%.

### Competitive Position

Old National competes with other mid-cap regional banks, but the Ameris acquisition moved it into a different competitive tier. Previously, it was competing primarily with FULT (Fulton Financial, $6.8B MC), WSBC (WesBanco, $6.5B MC), BUSE (First Busey, $5.0B MC), GABC (German American Bancorp), and NWBI (Northwest Bancshares, $3.5B MC). Post-acquisition, with $72.2B in assets, it sits closer to THFF (First Financial, $12.6B MC), FRME (First Merchants, $17.0B MC), and WTFC (Wintrust, $36.4B MC) in the asset category conversation.

The primary competitive advantage is geographic concentration. In its core markets — particularly Indiana and Ohio — Old National has deep branch penetration that creates switching costs and deposit stickiness. Banks in rural and small-town Midwest markets historically exhibit lower deposit turnover and higher fee capture rates than urban-focused banks. The Ameris addition extends this to parts of Georgia and the Carolinas.

The primary competitive vulnerability is scale. Even at $72.2B in assets, ONB lacks the technology infrastructure, treasury management sophistication, and cost-to-income efficiency of the large-cap money center banks (JPMorgan, Bank of America, PNC). Digital banking competition from fintech platforms (SoFi, Chime, Alkami) pressures deposit margins. If ONB cannot maintain its deposit franchise at competitive rates, it faces the classic regional-bank squeeze: higher funding costs without the scale to offset them through treasury management or capital markets fees.

### Moat Assessment

The moat is strongest in local-market deposit stickiness and branch presence in the Midwest, weaker in digital capability and scale efficiency, and the market appears to be fairly pricing the current P/B ratio relative to this assessment.

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

Reported earnings for Old National are reasonably clean, with some acquisition-related noise that is typical for the post-merger period. The key assessment:

The earnings are clean enough for valuation purposes because the revenue growth is largely interest-spread driven — not acquired, not adjusted, and not non-GAAP. Net interest income of $2.24 billion TTM is cash-based (funded by the balance sheet), not accrual-based, which means it is inherently more reliable than revenue growth from software bookings, channel stuffing, or one-time service contracts. The company's profit margin of 30.25% on TTM data is sustainable for a bank because it reflects the fundamental economics of the interest spread business after tax.

The biggest adjustment to consider is the Ameris acquisition itself. The FY2025 and TTM revenue growth includes the acquiredAmeris franchise, which means the "organic" revenue growth of the legacy Old National portfolio is harder to extract. If Ameris contributed approximately 25-30% of the revenue increase, the legacy franchise is still growing — but at a more modest rate of 5-8% rather than the headline 34%.

The key uncertainties are: (1) whether Ameris's loan portfolio performs at the same credit quality as the legacy ONB book, (2) whether the $230.5M in preferred stock carries meaningful dividend obligations that compress common equity returns, and (3) whether the $8.3B in total debt includes acquisition bridge financing that will need refinancing at market rates.

For valuation, I would use forward P/E on non-GAAP diluted EPS (which is what Yahoo Finance analysts are providing — $2.59 for FY26, $2.88 for FY27) and P/B as the primary metrics. Bank earnings are inherently "cleaner" than most because the revenue is interest income, which is cash-based, and the expenses are interest expense plus personnel/operations, which are well-documented.

## 9. Market Debate And Variant Perception

### What Bulls Believe

The bull case for Old National is that the Ameris acquisition was well-priced and the combined franchise will compound at above-average ROE levels. Bulls argue that:

The Midwest deposit franchise is one of the most stable in the country. Indiana, Ohio, and Michigan are not hotbeds of fintech adoption — customers use their local bank for decades. This creates a funding cost advantage: old money in low-cost accounts that doesn't reprice quickly when the Fed raises rates. The Ameris addition diversifies this with Southeast geographies that have higher growth rates.

The current P/B of 1.23x and forward P/E of 10.1x are below the sector averages. Regional banks that consistently earn 10%+ ROE typically trade at 1.30-1.60x book. If ONB demonstrates two clean years of ROE above 10% on the combined balance sheet, the P/B should expand, creating a multiple-expansion catalyst on top of the organic BVPS growth.

The consistent EPS beats across four quarters suggest that either management is conservative or the Ameris synergies are larger than models. Either way, the upside surprise potential is real.

### What Bears Believe

The bear case is that the Ameris acquisition masked credit deterioration that will become visible in 18-24 months. Bears argue that:

The 35% balance sheet expansion from the Ameris deal means the credit quality of nearly a quarter of the loan book is untested. Regional bank acquisitions historically see NPL ratios increase by 50-100 basis points in the first two post-merger years as underwriting standards converge and loan modifications emerge. If ONB's NPL jumps from the current 0.X% toward 1.0%+, the provision for credit losses will consume earnings and compress ROE.

The $8.3B in total debt needs scrutiny. If a significant portion is acquisition bridge financing maturing in 1-2 years, ONB will need to re-finance in a potentially higher-rate environment. The $230.5M in preferred stock also carries unknown dividend obligations that compress common equity returns.

The ROE of 10.08% is below the estimated cost of equity of 12.6%. A bank that earns less than its cost of equity is technically destroying shareholder value unless the P/B expands. At 1.23x P/B, there isn't much room for multiple expansion without a clear catalyst.

### What The Market May Be Missing

The market appears focused on whether ONB is "cheap" at 1.23x P/B and 10.1x forward P/E. The more important question is whether the combined loan portfolio's non-performing ratio and provision for credit losses track above or below the mid-cycle regional bank average. Most of the analyst coverage appears to be on the revenue and earnings trajectory, not on the credit quality dynamics. ## 11. Bear Case

At 0.90x P/B on a stagnating BVPS of $21-22, ONB would be worth approximately $19-20 per share — representing 25-30% downside from $27.29. The bear case is not revenue collapse — it's multiple compression on the same revenue base because the market re-prices the franchise risk after observing deteriorating credit metrics.

The mechanism is: rising NPLs → higher loan-loss provision → lower earnings → lower EPS versus consensus → multiple contraction. This is the standard regional bank stress pattern, exemplified by the 2008-2009 crisis where regional banks with 0.30% NPLs moved to 1.50%+ overnight.
