## 1. Thesis In One Page

### The Setup

T-Mobile US looks like an ex-growth telecom after a nearly 30% one-year share-price decline, but the operating record does not fit that label. TTM revenue grew 9.7%, operating income reached $20.37 billion, operating cash flow reached $28.83 billion, and free cash flow reached $18.40 billion. The share count fell 3.9% year over year. The business is still compounding service revenue, cash flow, and per-share value even as the market prices it at 13.43x forward adjusted earnings and 9.80x TTM FCF. Sources: StockAnalysis financials and statistics, September 18, 2026.

The real question is not whether T-Mobile has a good network or whether wireless service is recurring. The question is whether the company can convert its post-Sprint network advantage into durable per-share earnings while carrying $117.6 billion of net debt and distributing most of current FCF through buybacks and dividends. TTM repurchases of $12.39 billion and dividends of $4.34 billion consumed 91% of FCF. That policy is attractive while operations are strong and refinancing markets cooperate, but it leaves less room for debt reduction, spectrum purchases, fiber investment, or a competitive shock.

The stock is interesting because expectations have compressed more than the visible consensus. Analysts expect FY2026 revenue of $94.37 billion and adjusted EPS of $12.18, followed by FY2027 revenue of $98.60 billion and adjusted EPS of $14.44. At $168.18, the stock trades at only 11.6x the visible FY2027 adjusted estimate. The average analyst target is $243.38, 44.7% above the current price, while the low target of $169 is essentially the current quote. Sources: StockAnalysis forecast, last updated September 14, 2026.

The thesis works if network leadership keeps postpaid churn contained, broadband adds a second growth vector, operating leverage lifts cash flow, and buybacks reduce shares faster than debt and financing costs erode equity value. It fails if wireless pricing becomes promotional, fixed wireless reaches capacity, fiber expansion raises capital intensity, or management prioritizes distributions and acquisitions over balance-sheet resilience.

### The Core Debate

The bull answer is that T-Mobile has already paid for the hard part. Sprint provided spectrum, network scale, and a larger customer base; T-Mobile then integrated the network while expanding margins and FCF. Capex fell from $13.97 billion in 2022 to $9.96 billion in 2025 before rising modestly to $10.43 billion TTM. Over the same period, FCF rose from $2.81 billion to $18.40 billion. That is the operating harvest the original combination was supposed to create.

The bear answer is that the harvest is being capitalized aggressively. T-Mobile distributed $16.73 billion through buybacks and dividends over the last twelve months while cash fell to $2.83 billion and net debt remained $117.60 billion. The company can afford that today, but the equity becomes sensitive to interest rates, promotional competition, and capital-intensive broadband expansion. A low P/E is not automatically cheap when debt holders have a large prior claim on enterprise cash flow.

## 2. Company Story

T-Mobile US was founded in 1994 and evolved from VoiceStream Wireless into Deutsche Telekom's U.S. wireless platform. The modern investment story began when the company abandoned the conventional carrier playbook. Its “Un-carrier” strategy simplified plans, removed customer pain points, and used aggressive marketing to attract subscribers from larger rivals. The strategy mattered because wireless networks have high fixed costs: adding customers to an existing network can create strong incremental margins if service quality remains adequate.

The 2020 Sprint merger changed the scale and economics. Sprint brought customers and a deep mid-band spectrum portfolio, while T-Mobile brought stronger execution and a more effective consumer brand. The integration thesis was that one combined network could serve the two customer bases with lower duplicated operating and capital costs. The historical numbers support much of that thesis. Operating margin rose from 12.5% in 2021 to 22.1% TTM, and FCF margin rose from 2.0% to 20.0%. Sources: StockAnalysis financial overview through June 30, 2026.

The current chapter is different from the merger chapter. T-Mobile is no longer primarily an integration story. It is a capital-allocation and adjacency story. The company is returning cash through repurchases and dividends, expanding fixed wireless access, pursuing fiber, developing direct-to-device satellite coverage with SpaceX, and selling spectrum that no longer fits the network plan. Each decision asks whether management can extend the growth runway without recreating the capital intensity that the Sprint harvest reduced.

The company matters because mobile connectivity is essential infrastructure, but its competitive advantage is operational rather than regulatory monopoly. Customers can switch among T-Mobile, Verizon, and AT&T. The moat comes from network performance, spectrum position, brand, distribution, scale, and the practical inconvenience of switching multiple lines and devices. Those advantages reduce churn and acquisition cost; they do not eliminate price competition.

## 3. Business Model

T-Mobile earns most of its economics from recurring wireless service. Postpaid service is the core profit pool because customers pay monthly for smartphone, connected-device, and broadband access. Prepaid service addresses price-sensitive customers through different brands and distribution. Wholesale and other service revenue monetizes network access and partnerships. Equipment revenue is strategically useful for customer acquisition and retention but carries lower margins and can fluctuate with handset launches and promotional intensity.

TTM postpaid revenue was $61.74 billion, prepaid revenue was $10.20 billion, wholesale and other service revenue was $2.81 billion, equipment revenue was $16.35 billion, and other revenue was $1.08 billion. Postpaid represented roughly two-thirds of total revenue and a larger share of service economics. The mix explains why subscriber quality, churn, and average revenue per account matter more than reported equipment sales. Source: StockAnalysis financial overview through June 30, 2026.

The engine is: invest in spectrum and network capacity; attract and retain accounts with coverage, value, and brand; spread network costs across more connections; attach devices and broadband; convert service revenue into EBITDA and FCF; then use cash to repurchase shares, pay dividends, reduce debt, or reinvest. The weak link is capital intensity. Spectrum, towers, leases, equipment subsidies, and network upgrades require cash before customer economics fully arrive.

Wireless unit economics are disclosed indirectly rather than through a SaaS-style LTV/CAC ratio. Postpaid phone churn is the best retention proxy. Account growth and average revenue per account show volume and price. Cost per gross addition indicates acquisition intensity. Service-revenue margin and FCF indicate whether reported customer growth creates shareholder value. A carrier can report healthy adds while destroying value if promotions and subsidies exceed lifetime gross profit.

Fixed wireless access uses network capacity to sell home broadband without building a last-mile fiber connection to every home. The economics can be excellent where the mobile network has spare capacity: customer installation is simple, incremental capex is modest, and billing can attach to an existing relationship. The constraint is local capacity. As mobile traffic grows, the same spectrum cannot support unlimited home usage without degrading performance.

Fiber changes the equation. It broadens the addressable broadband market and can provide a durable high-capacity connection, but deployment is slower and more capital intensive. T-Mobile must prove that build cost, penetration, pricing, and churn produce attractive returns. Fiber should not be valued as “more broadband customers” without subtracting construction and customer-acquisition capital.

## 4. Products, Segments, And Moat

The core product is connectivity, not the handset. T-Mobile's branded postpaid plans bundle mobile data, voice, messaging, device financing, streaming benefits, international features, and customer-service promises. The company also sells prepaid access, home internet, business connectivity, wholesale capacity, devices, accessories, insurance-related services, and financing through equipment installment plans.

Postpaid drives the thesis because it combines recurring revenue with the strongest relationship. Family and business accounts often contain several lines, financed devices, and attached services. Switching requires coordinating users, device balances, number porting, coverage expectations, and employer or family preferences. Those frictions are real switching costs even when contracts are not formally long term.

|---|---:|---|---|
| Postpaid | $61.74B | Core recurring service and primary profit pool | Churn, promotions, price competition |
| Prepaid | $10.20B | Price-segment reach and brand portfolio | Lower loyalty and ARPU pressure |
| Wholesale & other services | $2.81B | Monetizes network and partnerships | Counterparty concentration and rate pressure |
| Equipment | $16.35B | Supports acquisition, retention, and ecosystem | Low margin, launch cycles, subsidies |
| Other | $1.08B | Ancillary services | Limited scale and disclosure |

The moat is strongest in network scale and spectrum. A national wireless network cannot be replicated cheaply or quickly. Spectrum licenses are scarce, tower relationships take time, and network optimization requires operating experience. T-Mobile's Sprint-derived mid-band holdings helped it deploy broad 5G coverage with a balance of reach and capacity. That position supported marketing claims and customer migration.

The moat is weaker in product differentiation. Competitors can copy plan features, trade-in offers, streaming bundles, and price guarantees. Verizon and AT&T have national networks, large installed bases, enterprise relationships, and substantial FCF. Cable operators can bundle wireless with home broadband through MVNO arrangements. The customer sees meaningful network differences in some locations, but many urban users experience acceptable service from all three national carriers.

Satellite direct-to-device creates both option value and ambiguity. T-Mobile can use satellite coverage to address dead zones and emergency connectivity without building terrestrial infrastructure everywhere. That strengthens coverage marketing. Yet a partner with growing spectrum, launch capability, and consumer ambition could eventually seek more of the economics or become a competitor. The partnership should be underwritten as differentiated coverage, not as a permanent exclusive moat.

The best evidence of moat is economic: service growth, low churn, margins, and cash conversion. TTM gross margin was 63.05%, operating margin was 22.09%, and FCF margin was 19.96%. Those figures are substantially better than the early post-merger period. The market may be underrating how much of the integration advantage is now visible in cash, but it may also be correctly anticipating that future gains become harder after the obvious synergies are harvested.

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

Earnings quality is **good on cash conversion, but noisy between operating income and GAAP net income**. TTM OCF of $28.83 billion was 2.73x net income of $10.56 billion. FCF of $18.40 billion was 1.74x net income after $10.43 billion of capex. Depreciation and amortization of $14.01 billion is the main bridge because network and spectrum investments create large non-cash expense. Source: StockAnalysis cash flow through June 30, 2026.

The cash conversion is not automatically excess economic profit. Telecom depreciation represents real network assets that must eventually be replaced or upgraded. The appropriate owner-earnings deduction is maintenance capex, not zero capex. Reported FCF subtracts all capex, including growth and capacity spending, which makes it a useful conservative anchor even if annual timing varies.

Working capital reduced TTM cash by $2.48 billion, primarily through receivables and other operating assets. That argues against cash flow being inflated by a one-time working-capital release. Stock-based compensation was $866 million, only 3% of OCF. Bad-debt provision and write-offs were $1.61 billion, a meaningful amount that should be monitored as handset financing and consumer credit normalize.

The weaker feature is the divergence between operating and net results. Operating income remained near $20.4 billion while net income declined. Cash interest paid was $4.13 billion, and the TTM effective tax rate was 23.62%. Restructuring and asset-writedown costs were $688 million. None alone breaks the thesis, but the company must show that adjusted EPS growth is not achieved mainly by excluding recurring transformation costs.

Consensus EPS is explicitly non-GAAP adjusted. StockAnalysis shows FY2026 adjusted EPS of $12.18 versus TTM GAAP EPS of $9.54. The 28% gap may include timing, growth, and adjustments. Investors should not compare the 13.43x forward P/E with GAAP peers without checking definitions. This report uses adjusted EPS for scenario comparability, then checks the result against FCF and EV/EBITDA.

Capital returns make per-share earnings cleaner in one sense and more financialized in another. Repurchases reduce the denominator, so EPS can rise faster than total net income. That is genuine value creation when shares are purchased below intrinsic value and the balance sheet remains sound. It is financial engineering when funded by debt or when operational investment is deferred. T-Mobile's current price makes buybacks economically attractive, but the 91% FCF distribution rate leaves little margin for error.

FY2027 revenue is expected to reach $98.60 billion, up 4.49%, while adjusted EPS reaches $14.44, up 18.58%. The divergence between revenue and EPS growth is central. Consensus assumes operating leverage, share reduction, or lower below-the-line costs can produce roughly four times the EPS growth rate of revenue in FY2027. That is plausible after Sprint integration and at the current buyback pace. It is not indefinitely sustainable. Once network synergies mature and the share base is smaller, EPS growth must increasingly come from service revenue, pricing, broadband, and disciplined capital intensity.

The base case begins with FY2027 consensus rather than inventing a near-term estimate. It applies 15x to $14.44 of adjusted EPS for $216.60. A 15x multiple is modest for high-teens EPS growth, but appropriate for a leveraged, mature telecom whose long-run revenue growth is likely mid-single-digit. The base target is 11% below the analyst average, providing a calibration discount.

The $133.08 target is 20.9% below the current price. The bear does not assume insolvency or revenue collapse; it assumes T-Mobile earns less and is valued like a mature carrier.

The bull uses $16.00 and 17x for $272.00. It requires continued network leadership, healthy broadband economics, cost control, and buybacks without a balance-sheet penalty. The target remains below the $300 analyst high, avoiding a scenario that simply imports the most optimistic sell-side view.

## 8. Management, Governance, And Capital Allocation

Management's strongest evidence is execution. The company integrated Sprint's network assets, expanded margins, reduced capex from the merger peak, and generated a step-change in FCF. Those results deserve more weight than branding claims. Operating income doubled from 2021, and annual FCF increased from $1.59 billion to $18.40 billion TTM.

Capital allocation has shifted from integration toward distributions. TTM buybacks were $12.39 billion and dividends were $4.34 billion. The current annual dividend is $4.08 per share, yielding 2.43%, and grew 15.9% year over year. The share count fell 3.9%. Those actions create visible shareholder returns and signal confidence in cash generation.

The concern is sequencing. Debt and leases remain large, cash is low, and fiber can require sustained investment. Repurchasing stock at 11-13x forward earnings is attractive, but not if it forces refinancing or underinvestment at a bad time. Management should express capital returns as residual after network quality, liquidity, and leverage—not as fixed promises independent of conditions.

The 800 MHz spectrum sale completed in August 2026 is a positive example of recycling. Spectrum that does not fit the network plan can be sold and redeployed. Investors need the consideration, accounting gain, tax effect, and use of proceeds to evaluate whether the transaction reduced leverage or simply funded distributions.

CFO Peter Osvaldik is scheduled to step down in February 2027, with Jessica Uhl succeeding him. CFO transitions matter more in a highly leveraged, capital-intensive company than in a net-cash software business. The new CFO will influence debt maturities, hedging, spectrum financing, fiber commitments, and the balance between repurchases and deleveraging. Continuity is not automatically good or bad; the relevant question is whether the leverage framework becomes clearer.

Governance also includes Deutsche Telekom's ownership and strategic interest. Reports that Elliott opposed a potential Deutsche Telekom/T-Mobile transaction highlight control and minority-holder questions. Any merger, increased stake, or reorganization should be assessed for process, valuation, conflicts, and protections rather than assumed to create a premium.

## 9. Market Debate And Variant Perception

Bulls see the best operator in U.S. wireless at a mature-carrier multiple. The network advantage created by Sprint spectrum is producing service growth and cash. Fixed wireless extends the network into broadband at attractive incremental economics. Buybacks shrink the share count at a low multiple. In this view, the market has mistaken temporary competitive and satellite fears for structural deterioration.

Bears see a mature wireless market with three rational competitors that can become irrational when subscriber growth slows. Promotions transfer value to customers through free devices and switching credits. Cable companies bundle wireless with broadband. Satellite technology threatens to alter coverage economics. Fiber expansion risks turning a high-return FWA adjacency into another capital-heavy fixed-network strategy.

The stock's position near the bottom of its $164.02-$242.37 52-week range amplifies the debate. The multiple compression is already substantial, but a low price does not stop momentum. Further downside requires either lower estimates, higher financing risk, or a structural multiple reset. Conversely, returning to the old high without earnings growth would demand a re-rating that is not necessary for the base case.

The variant perception is that FCF durability matters more than subscriber headlines. T-Mobile can tolerate slower gross adds if churn remains controlled, service revenue grows, and capex stays near the current level. It cannot support the current distribution program if promotions, fiber, and spectrum simultaneously consume cash. The decisive KPI is FCF after sustaining network quality—not one quarter of account adds.

Another variant is that satellite may be more complementary than disruptive in the medium term. Direct-to-device coverage is valuable for dead zones and emergencies but does not yet replace dense terrestrial capacity for everyday urban data. The immediate economic question is partnership structure and spectrum use, not whether satellites suddenly eliminate towers. The long-term competitive possibility deserves monitoring without assuming an imminent substitute.

## 10. Bull Case

The bull case starts with network retention. T-Mobile preserves a measurable 5G performance advantage, postpaid churn remains low, and the company continues taking high-quality accounts without buying them through uneconomic promotions. Service revenue grows faster than the broader wireless market because customer growth and ARPA both contribute.

Fixed wireless remains a high-return use of excess capacity. The company adds broadband customers selectively by geography, protecting mobile performance and avoiding the need to overbuild solely for home internet. Fiber expands in markets where build partners, density, and penetration support attractive returns rather than as a national vanity project.

Operating leverage continues. Revenue reaches the visible $98.60 billion FY2027 consensus and grows thereafter at mid-single digits. Network and back-office simplification offset wage, energy, and customer-acquisition costs. Adjusted EPS reaches $16.00 as total earnings rise and the share count falls.

Cash flow supports all claims. FCF remains near or above 20% of revenue, distributions remain covered, and spectrum-sale proceeds or excess cash reduce net debt. Interest coverage improves rather than merely holding. A 17x multiple on $16.00 produces $272.00, still below the high analyst target.

The causal chain is: network performance lowers churn; lower churn reduces replacement acquisition cost; service growth flows through a high fixed-cost network; FCF funds undervalued buybacks; shares fall; per-share earnings grow faster than revenue; and lower leverage allows a higher multiple. The bull fails if buybacks are debt-funded or if broadband requires substantially more capital than assumed.

## 11. Bear Case

The bear case does not require a technological collapse. It begins with a mature market and aggressive promotions. Verizon, AT&T, and cable competitors spend more to defend or gain accounts. T-Mobile responds with handset credits and plan incentives. Customer growth continues, but acquisition cost rises and revenue quality declines.

Fixed wireless approaches local capacity limits in attractive markets. Management adds fewer customers or spends more to expand capacity. Fiber becomes strategically important but economically dilutive during the build phase. Capex rises while the revenue benefit arrives slowly.

Below-the-line pressure persists. Debt refinancing occurs at higher rates than the embedded 3.4% cash cost, restructuring charges continue, and adjusted-to-GAAP differences remain wide. Management maintains distributions too long, leaving net debt elevated. The market values the company as a mature leveraged carrier rather than as a growth compounder.

Adjusted EPS lands near the visible low estimate of $11.09 and receives a 12x multiple. The $133.08 target implies 20.9% downside. This is plausible despite healthy FCF because enterprise claims absorb much of the value and because a lower multiple can offset buyback-driven EPS support.

Warning signals would include postpaid churn rising for several quarters, service revenue growth falling below inflation, promotional equipment economics weakening, FWA net additions slowing without fiber offset, capex moving materially above $11 billion, FCF falling below $16 billion, or net debt increasing while repurchases continue.

## 12. Base Case

The base case assumes T-Mobile remains the strongest growth operator in a mature industry, but that its advantage narrows. Revenue reaches $94.37 billion in FY2026 and $98.60 billion in FY2027, consistent with visible consensus. Growth slows toward 4-5% as equipment comparisons and merger effects normalize.

Adjusted EPS reaches $14.44 in FY2027. Operating leverage, buybacks, and service mix allow EPS to grow faster than revenue, but the gap narrows after 2027. The company maintains a roughly 20% FCF margin through disciplined capex and expense control.

Capital allocation becomes more balanced. Buybacks continue because the stock is inexpensive, but management also uses transaction proceeds and part of annual FCF to reduce net debt. Dividend growth moderates from the current mid-teens pace. Fiber investment is phased and partnered rather than funded as an unconstrained national build.

At 15x adjusted EPS, fair value is $216.60. The multiple recognizes recurring revenue, network quality, and per-share growth while discounting leverage and maturity. It is below the analyst average target because the report does not assume that the prior premium returns automatically.

The base case is the most likely outcome because current operations do not show distress, while the capital structure prevents an unqualified bullish stance. The next quarterly update should change probabilities more than terminal assumptions: strong churn and FCF increase the base/bull weights; rising capex or weaker service economics increase the bear weight.

T-Mobile's equity valuation is inexpensive on earnings and cash flow. The stock trades at 17.63x TTM GAAP EPS, 13.43x forward adjusted EPS, 1.96x sales, and 9.80x FCF. The 10.2% FCF yield is attractive for a business with recurring service revenue and visible share reduction. Source: StockAnalysis statistics, September 18, 2026.

Enterprise valuation is less obviously cheap. EV is $298.0 billion, or 3.23x sales, 8.67x EBITDA, and 16.20x FCF. The difference between 9.80x P/FCF and 16.20x EV/FCF is the capital structure. Equity investors should not quote the first without understanding the second.

Forward P/E is the cleanest primary framework because visible consensus provides adjusted EPS and the company actively changes the share count. It captures per-share benefit from buybacks. Its weakness is that adjusted EPS can exclude recurring costs and does not penalize debt-funded repurchases enough. EV/EBITDA and FCF provide the necessary balance.

The scenario target of $209.57 is not a DCF point estimate. It is a probability-weighted decision aid. Below the bear value, the market would price significant operational disappointment. Around $215-$220, it would price a clean consensus outcome. Above $270, investors would require sustained EPS growth, lower leverage risk, or a restored premium multiple.

The analyst average target of $243.38 is directionally supportive but not evidence of intrinsic value. It sits 12.4% above the model's base and 16.1% above weighted value. That gap is reasonable because this report assigns explicit probability to a bear case and discounts leverage more heavily than an earnings-only target can.

The reverse valuation provides the strongest entry argument. At 11.65x FY2027 consensus adjusted EPS, the current price does not need multiple expansion for a modest positive total return if consensus holds. The risk is estimate durability: an apparent low multiple can disappear quickly if adjusted EPS is cut toward the bear anchor.

## 14. Catalysts

The next quarterly release is the main catalyst. StockAnalysis lists an estimated October 22 earnings date, while a September 17 company announcement says T-Mobile will host its Q3 2026 earnings call on October 28. The confirmed reporting schedule should be checked directly. The update must be judged on postpaid service revenue, phone churn, broadband additions, capex, and FCF—not simply EPS versus consensus.

2027 capital-allocation guidance is the second catalyst. Investors need a hierarchy among network investment, fiber, spectrum, debt, dividends, and buybacks. A leverage guardrail would reduce the risk that distributions remain fixed through a weaker operating period.

The CFO transition in February 2027 is a governance catalyst. Jessica Uhl can increase confidence by preserving reporting quality, explaining debt maturities and lease obligations, and articulating return thresholds for fiber. A vague commitment to both maximum growth and maximum capital returns would be less credible.

Spectrum-sale disclosure can create a smaller catalyst. The completed 800 MHz transaction may produce cash, a gain, or network simplification. The market should distinguish recurring earnings from transaction accounting and observe whether proceeds reduce debt or fund distributions.

Competitive behavior remains an external catalyst. Handset launches and holiday promotions can change gross-add economics quickly. A rational promotional environment supports the base case; a subsidy escalation supports the bear.

## 15. Risks And Red Flags

The biggest risk is the interaction of leverage and capital returns. T-Mobile can support $16.7 billion of annual distributions while FCF is $18.4 billion, but that coverage is thin after considering spectrum, acquisitions, refinancing, or unexpected network investment. Net debt of $117.6 billion removes flexibility precisely when a downturn or technology transition could make flexibility most valuable.

Competition is the second risk. Wireless service is recurring, but customers can switch. Device promotions, price guarantees, and bundled broadband offers can raise acquisition cost or reduce ARPA. Because the network has high fixed costs, small revenue or churn changes can produce larger changes in incremental profit.

Broadband strategy is the third risk. FWA is attractive because it monetizes existing capacity, but its success can consume the capacity that made it attractive. Fiber solves capacity but requires construction capital. Management could move from an asset-efficient adjacency to a lower-return build without investors recognizing the change soon enough.

Technology is a two-sided risk. Satellite direct-to-device improves coverage and resilience, but technology and partnership economics can evolve. Network virtualization and AI may reduce cost, while cyber threats increase operational and reputational exposure. Telecom infrastructure is a national-security target; outages or breaches can create regulatory and customer consequences.

Intangible concentration is another red flag. Goodwill and other intangibles total $115.1 billion, more than twice common equity. Spectrum is valuable, but reported book value is not a liquidation floor. Changes in technology, regulation, or strategy can impair assets even if cash operations remain healthy.

Other risks include regulatory restrictions, spectrum-auction costs, tower and lease inflation, consumer credit losses, equipment-financing defaults, labor expense, supply-chain concentration, handset-vendor dependence, and a Deutsche Telekom transaction that does not treat minority holders fairly.

## 16. What Would Change My Mind

I would become more bullish if T-Mobile reports sustained postpaid service growth with stable or lower churn, keeps capex near $10-$11 billion, maintains FCF at or above $18 billion, and reduces net debt despite continued buybacks. A clear fiber return framework and evidence that FWA remains capacity-disciplined would increase confidence. Confirmed FY2027 adjusted EPS near $14.44 without widening adjustments would support the base target.

I would move toward Own if the stock remains near the current price while those operating conditions are met and management commits to a leverage ceiling. The low forward multiple would then represent pessimism rather than compensation for an uncontrolled balance sheet.

I would become more bearish if FCF falls below $16 billion, net debt rises, or capex accelerates without a proportionate service-revenue benefit. Rising churn, weaker account economics, or a large gap between adjusted EPS and GAAP/cash results would challenge earnings quality. Maintaining repurchases through those conditions would be a governance negative.

I would abandon the thesis if network quality loses its advantage, service revenue contracts outside a recession, interest coverage approaches 3x, or management undertakes a debt-funded strategic transaction that prioritizes scale over per-share returns.

## 17. Monitoring Checklist

The monitoring framework should emphasize economics rather than headlines. Subscriber additions matter only with churn, pricing, promotions, and FCF. Broadband growth matters only with capacity and capital intensity. Buybacks matter only with leverage and purchase price.

## 18. Conclusion

T-Mobile is a strong operator trading at an attractive equity multiple, but it is not a simple low-risk compounder. The business has converted Sprint integration into real margin and FCF gains. Revenue, operating income, cash flow, and per-share value are moving in the right direction. At 11.65x visible FY2027 adjusted EPS, the market prices substantial skepticism.

The model's probability-weighted fair value is $209.57, with a $216.60 base case and 24.6% weighted upside. The main reason to care is the combination of recurring service revenue, a 10.2% FCF yield, and active share reduction. The main risk is that $117.6 billion of net debt and a 91% FCF distribution rate reduce resilience just as fiber, satellite, and competitive spending create new capital demands.

The stance is Watch. The next trigger is the October quarterly update and 2027 capital-allocation framework. A clean report with stable churn, strong FCF, disciplined fiber investment, and a leverage guardrail would make the current valuation compelling. Weaker cash conversion or continued maximum distributions without deleveraging would make the low multiple deserved.
