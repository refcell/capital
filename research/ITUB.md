## 1. Thesis In One Page

### The Setup

Itaú Unibanco is Brazil's largest bank by assets (3.066 trillion BRL as of FY2025) and one of the largest listed financial institutions in Latin America. The stock trades at 9.55x trailing P/E, 9.43x forward P/E, and 2.03x P/B — all reasonably close to historic averages. ROE of 21.48% is structurally strong for a large diversified bank. The story is not that the bank is cheap in a fire sale; the stock is that there is room for BVPS to compound through retained earnings and buybacks at a reasonable multiple.

The real question is whether the ROE of 21% is sustainable or cyclical, whether Brazil's high-rate environment (SELIC at ~10.5%) is a tailwind or a headwind for net interest margins, and whether the 21% ROE can be sustained through a rate-cycle downturn.

In the short term, the thesis works if NIM stabilizes and credit losses remain contained. It fails if Brazil economic deteriorates credit losses surge and NIM compresses from rate declines. The stock is interesting because it offers a quality Brazilian large-cap franchise with a reasonable valuation and a low beta (0.16) that acts as a portfolio diversifier — but it is not compelling enough to own outright without closer inspection of credit trajectory.

### The Core Debate

The debate is not whether Itaú is profitable — the bank generates 46.83 billion BRL in net income TTM and returns 21.48% on equity. It is whether the ROE can be sustained through normalizing credit cycle. Brazil's high interest rate environment inflates NIM, but higher rates also increase default risk. If the ROE normalizes toward 18%, the stock could underperform significantly at current P/B. The other side is that Itaú's market-leading scale, diversified revenue mix, and technology investment insulate it from the kind of credit deterioration that smaller Brazilian banks face.

## 2. Company Story

### Origin And Evolution

Itaú Unibanco traces its roots to the Itaú Financial Holding group, which was founded in 1924 in São Paulo. The modern entity emerged from the 2008 combination of Banco Itaú and Banco Unibanco — a merger-of-equals that created Brazil's largest bank by assets. The combination was complex: Unibanco brought a strong retail deposit franchise and Itaú brought corporate banking and wealth management depth. Post-merger integration was largely successful, creating a bank that dominates Brazilian banking across retail, corporate, investment banking, and wealth management.

The company underwent additional consolidation in Latin America, though it has focused increasingly on Brazil itself rather than the broader region. The company also has a wealth management subsidiary (Itaú BBA) that competes in the Brazilian institutional market and has expanded fintech partnerships and digital banking capabilities. The company announced a 103:100 stock split on December 29, 2025 — a routine liquidity enhancement that had no economic impact on per-share value.

### Why The Company Matters

Itaú Unibanco processes a third of all financial transactions in Brazil. The Brazilian economy accounts for roughly 25% of GDP in the Americas. For Brazilian consumers and businesses, banking through Itaú's network provides access to deposits, credit, payments, wealth management, and insurance — often integrated on a single digital platform. The company matters because it is a gateway to the Brazilian economy: when Brazil grows, Itaú's revenues grow across lending, deposits, and fee income. When Brazil struggles, the exposure runs both ways. The company is also a key employer in Brazil and a significant contributor to Brazilian tax revenues.

## 3. Business Model

### How The Company Makes Money

Banking revenue for Itaú comes from three primary channels: (1) Net interest income — the spread between what the bank earns on loans and investments and what it pays on deposits and borrowings. Brazil's high interest rate environment (SELIC ~10.5%) drives wide NIM, but it also comes with the offset that higher rates increase default risk. (2) Fee and commission income — wealth management fees, transaction fees, credit card interchange income, and payment processing. This is lower-yielding but fee income is also the least cyclical revenue stream. (3) Trading income and other — FX trading, securities underwriting, and treasury operations contribute meaningfully given Brazil's currency volatility.

The bank's revenue structure is weighted toward net interest income, which means profitability is highly correlated with Brazil's interest rate trajectory. The current 32.6% net profit margin is the bank's conversion of revenue (including NII) to bottom-line earnings — high for a bank and speaks to cost discipline, but the conversion ratio includes provisions for credit losses, interest expense, and taxes.

### The Economic Engine

The engine is: acquire low-cost deposits → underwrite credit and expand loan book → manage losses through underwriting discipline → earn net interest spread → retain earnings and compound book value per share. The weak link is credit quality in a high-rate Brazil. When rate cuts reduce NIM, loan quality may deteriorate simultaneously — compressing ROE from both sides of the equation. The ROE also depends on capital discipline: 21.5% ROE on 204.5B BRL of common equity is structurally strong, but sustainability is unproven through a rate-cycle downturn.

### Unit Economics / Customer Economics

Banking unit economics are not typically broken out at the customer level. The relevant proxies are: deposit funding cost (very low for retail), loan yield (high due to Brazil's interest rate environment), NIM spread (the key metric), cost-to-income ratio (operating efficiency), and loan loss provision relative to gross loans. ITU's ROE of 21.5% implies that the bank is compounding book value at that rate on retained earnings — which is compelling if sustainable. The payout ratio of 74% means only 26% of earnings are retained, meaning BVPS compounds at roughly 21.5% × 26% = 5.6% annually from retained earnings alone, plus additional BVPS gains from buybacks.

### Competitive Position

Itá leads Brazil by assets, market share, and network scale. The primary competitors are Banco Bradesco (BB), Banco Santander Brasil (BSBR), and the rapidly growing digital bank Nu Holdings (NU). Itaú's advantage comes from: (1) first-mover advantage in digital transformation — it captured the mobile banking shift ahead of competitors during 2020-2022, (2) deposit franchise depth — Itaú holds more retail deposits than any Brazilian bank, which creates low-cost funding, and (3) integrated platform — ITá offers retail, corporate, wealth management, and payments under one digital umbrella.

The competitive risk is fintech disintermediation. Nu Holdings has grown rapidly by offering digital-first banking at zero fees and lower spreads. Over the long term, fintech competition compresses NIM across the industry. However, Itaú's own digital investments (Itaú's digital platform) have been substantial and it has been ahead of most competitors.

### Moat Assessment

The moat is strongest in scale and deposit franchise. Itaú's network of branches, digital platform, and customer data creates a switching cost for customers — switching banks in Brazil is inconvenient and ITú's brand recognition is deeply embedded in consumer behavior. The moat is weaker in competitive pricing: Brazil's banking industry faces fintech competition that can undercut traditional margins. The market may be underrating Itaú because the ADR trades in a small float with limited U.S. analyst coverage — the P/B of 2.03x is at the lower end for a bank with 21% ROE.

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

The earnings quality assessment starts with whether reported earnings approximate owner earnings. For Itaú, the assessment is: **Clean, with structural banking accounting considerations.**

Reported earnings are largely clean. Net income is cash-backed: operating cash flow TTM of 159B BRL exceeds net income TTM of 46.83B BRL by a 3.4x multiple, which is typical for a bank — OCF reflects working capital movements around loan origination and deposit accumulation, not the same as "cash from operations" in an industrial company. Net income conversion is reasonable: the 32.6% net margin is sustainable for a well-run bank in Brazil's high-rate environment.

The biggest adjustment is provisions for credit losses. Banks must set aside provisions that may vary quarter-to-quarter based on underwriting conditions. ITá's credit quality metrics will need continued monitoring. The key uncertainty is whether the current ROE of 21.5% can be sustained if Brazil's credit cycle worsens. For valuation, I would use P/B and BVPS trajectory rather than earnings multiples — the P/B framework is more stable across business cycles for banks because book value is audited quarterly and less subject to management discretion than retained earnings.

The tax rate of ~12.4% is below Brazil's statutory corporate rate — this is consistent with preferential tax regimes (IRRF) available to large Brazilian financial institutions. This is a structural feature, not an accounting anomaly.
