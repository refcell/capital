## 1. Thesis In One Page

### The Setup

Primoris is a $7 billion-revenue contractor that builds and maintains the physical grid: power lines, substations, gas distribution, utility-scale solar and battery plants, natural-gas generation, and, since May 2026, electrical construction for data centers and factories. Until this year it was one of the clean winners of the power build-out. Revenue rose from $3.50 billion in 2021 to $7.57 billion in 2025, GAAP diluted EPS rose from $2.17 to $5.02, and the stock ended 2025 at $124.14 against $33.21 two years earlier (year-end monthly closes, Yahoo Finance). It peaked at $202.92 on May 5, 2026.

Then the renewables business broke. The Q1 release on May 5 described "cost pressures on a limited number of renewables projects" and the stock closed at $101.23 the next day, down 50.1%. On June 22 the company said a third-party expert had found more overruns on six projects, cut FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance from $4.80–$5.00 to $2.05–$2.60, cut adjusted EBITDA from $480–$500 million to $275–$325 million, and announced that the chief operating officer had left; the stock fell 21.6% to $84.95. Q2 then showed an Energy segment gross margin of minus 0.3% and a net loss of $24.2 million. On November 3 Q3 looked like a recovery quarter (Energy gross margin back to 8.5%, record backlog of $14.1 billion), but the full-year range was narrowed to $2.10–$2.25, which implies a weak Q4, and the stock fell from $79.57 to $59.56 over two sessions before bouncing to $71.24 on November 9.

The question for an investor is no longer whether Primoris had a bad year. It did. The question is what normal earnings look like once the six projects are finished, and how long it takes to get there.

### The Core Debate

The bull reading is that 2026 is a one-time cleanup. Five of the six problem projects were scheduled to be substantially complete by the end of Q3 and the last in Q4. Utilities, the steadier half of the company, grew operating income 27.2% in Q3 at a 13.0% gross margin. Backlog is a record $14.07 billion, up $2.1 billion since December, with $8.50 billion of it in master service agreements that renew with utilities. Data-center and gas-generation work is filling the Energy backlog. If FY2027 adjusted EPS gets back to the Street's $5.32, $71 is 13.4x a normal year.

The bear reading is that the problem is not six projects but the solar business itself. Management has said three times this year that renewables revenue is falling because of "slower than anticipated start of new projects and release of new work," and expects about $2.1 billion of renewables revenue in 2026 against $3.0 billion in 2025. Lower volume leaves equipment and crews underused, which the Q3 release named as a margin drag. A contractor that misbid six projects in one cycle can misbid again, and the balance sheet that held $535.5 million of cash in December now holds $203.5 million after a $404.7 million acquisition and $150 million of buybacks.

## 2. Company Story

### Origin And Evolution

Primoris traces its operating roots to ARB, Inc., a California pipeline and underground construction contractor founded in 1960, and became a public company in 2008 through a merger with a blank-check company, Rhapsody Acquisition Corp. It is now headquartered in Dallas. For most of its public life it was a mid-cap, low-multiple contractor whose results swung with pipeline and industrial cycles: GAAP diluted EPS was $0.51 in 2016, $1.50 in 2018 and $2.47 in 2022 (SEC XBRL).

Growth came in steps through acquisitions. Revenue rose 23.5% in 2018, the year Primoris bought the pipeline and utility contractor Willbros; 26.4% in 2022, the year it bought the power-delivery contractor PLH Group; and 29.3% in 2023 as PLH annualized and solar volume grew. Those deal names and years come from company history rather than from filings saved for this run; the revenue steps themselves are SEC-reported. By 2024 the company had simplified into two segments, Utilities and Energy, and its solar business had become one of the largest utility-scale solar builders in the United States.

The 2024–2025 run is what changed the stock. Revenue grew 11.4% in 2024 and 19.0% in 2025, operating margin widened from 4.4% in 2023 to 5.4% in 2025, and the market began to value Primoris as a beneficiary of the electricity build-out for data centers rather than as a general contractor. Year-end trailing P/E on GAAP EPS went from 14.3x at the end of 2023 to 23.1x at the end of 2024 and 24.7x at the end of 2025.

### Why The Company Matters

Primoris matters because it sits on both sides of the power problem. Its Utilities segment does recurring maintenance and upgrade work on transmission and distribution lines and gas systems under multiyear master service agreements, which is the steady part of utility capital spending. Its Energy segment builds new generation: solar farms and batteries, gas-fired plants, and now the electrical systems inside data centers and factories. That gives it exposure to the fastest-growing category of US construction without being a pure bet on one fuel.

The weakness of that position is the contract form. New generation is mostly fixed-price engineering, procurement and construction work. When a contractor misjudges labor productivity, weather, interconnection timing or subcontractor costs on a fixed-price job, it eats the overrun. That is what happened in 2026.

## 3. Business Model

### How The Company Makes Money

Primoris sells labor, equipment and project management. In Utilities it is paid mostly under master service agreements: a utility awards a multi-year framework, then releases work orders for line maintenance, pole replacement, substation work, gas main replacement or storm restoration, billed at unit rates or on a cost-plus basis. The company reports $8.01 billion of Utilities MSA backlog at September 30, 2026, up from $6.33 billion in December 2025, which is the estimated value of those frameworks over their remaining terms. Utilities revenue was $2.12 billion in the first nine months of 2026 at an 11.7% gross margin.

In Energy it is paid mostly on fixed-price or fixed-unit-price contracts to build specific projects. Energy revenue was $3.18 billion in the first nine months at a 5.5% gross margin, down from $3.83 billion at 10.5% a year earlier. Energy fixed backlog was $5.50 billion at September 30, including about $593.6 million from PayneCrest, with $3.71 billion expected to be worked off in the next twelve months. Revenue is recognized over time as costs are incurred, so a project that is overrunning books its expected loss when the overrun is estimated, not when the project ends.

### The Economic Engine

The engine is crew and equipment utilization. A contractor's gross margin is the spread between what it bids and what it costs to keep trained crews and owned equipment busy; selling and administrative cost is mostly fixed at about 5–6% of revenue. When volume is high and projects run to plan, gross margin of 10–12% turns into operating margin of 5–6%, and because capital spending is modest (about $130 million in 2025, 1.7% of revenue), most of the profit converts to cash over a cycle.

When volume falls, the same fixed cost base bites twice. The Q3 2026 release says Energy had "lower than anticipated volumes in 2026, which led to higher relative carrying costs for equipment and personnel." Selling and administrative cost rose to 5.6% of revenue in Q3 from 4.5% a year earlier. That is why a 10% revenue decline cut Q3 operating income by 30.7%.

### Unit Economics / Customer Economics

Primoris does not report project-level economics, but the segment numbers show the spread between the two contract forms. Utilities gross margin has stayed between about 9.2% and 14.1% by quarter through 2025–2026, and its operating margin was 9.1% in Q3 2026. Energy gross margin swung from 10.8% in Q2 2025 to minus 0.3% in Q2 2026 and back to 8.5% in Q3 2026. The steady half earns less on peak but never loses money; the project half earns more on peak but can wipe out a year.

Working capital is the other unit-economics variable. Contractors bill in arrears and hold retainage; accounts receivable plus contract assets were $1.82 billion at September 30, 2026, about 94 days of trailing revenue, versus $1.66 billion and about 80 days at December 31, 2025. Some of that is PayneCrest; some is likely billing delays and claims on the troubled projects. It is the main reason nine-month operating cash flow was only $8.9 million.

### Competitive Position

Primoris competes with Quanta Services, MasTec, MYR Group, Dycom, and a long tail of regional contractors in Utilities, and with Quanta, MasTec, Blattner (owned by Quanta), McCarthy, Mortenson, Rosendin and others in utility-scale solar. It is a top-tier solar builder by megawatts installed but a smaller player in transmission than Quanta. In utility MSAs the competitive edge is local crews, safety record and relationship tenure; utilities rarely switch an incumbent that performs. In solar EPC the edge is price and execution, and the barriers are lower: developers rebid every project.

### Moat Assessment

The Utilities business has a narrow moat built on incumbency. MSA backlog rose 27% in nine months, and Utilities gross margin improved to 13.0% in Q3, which suggests pricing power and good crew utilization. The Energy business has no durable moat. It has scale and a reputation, and both were dented this year. The 2026 losses are evidence that Primoris was competing on price in solar in 2024–2025, when developers were rushing to start projects before federal tax-credit deadlines and contractors were scarce.

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

Earnings quality in 2026 is noisy, and the noise runs in both directions. GAAP earnings are depressed by real cash losses on six projects; adjusted earnings are flattered by add-backs that are now larger and more permanent than before.

Start with the add-backs. Adjusted net income adds back stock compensation (about $20.5 million in 2026), transaction and integration costs ($9.1 million), severance, and amortization of acquired intangibles. The amortization line is the one that changed: $13.5 million in Q3 2026 against $4.3 million a year earlier, and $41.5 million guided for the full year, because of PayneCrest. Stock compensation is a real recurring cost and amortization reflects a real purchase price. For FY2026 the add-backs total $73.7 million pre-tax and lift EPS from about $1.28 GAAP to about $2.18 adjusted at the guide midpoints, which is why this report translates every adjusted multiple back to GAAP.

The cash flow is weaker than the income statement. Nine-month operating cash flow was $8.9 million against $44.6 million of net income and $88.5 million of depreciation and amortization. Payables fell $73.5 million and receivables rose $116.5 million while contract assets fell $81.1 million. The likely story is that the 2024–2025 solar boom pulled cash forward through advances and favorable payment terms, and the troubled projects are now consuming it. If any of the $880.6 million of contract assets includes unapproved change orders or claims on the six projects, a part of the 2026 loss may still be ahead. The Q3 release does not quantify claims; the 10-Q note on contract estimates is the place to check.

There is one item in the other direction. Gains on sale of equipment were $14.5 million in the first nine months (2025: $18.7 million). That is normal for a contractor that rotates its fleet, but it is a non-operating source of profit that will shrink if the company keeps more equipment idle rather than selling it.

Verdict: GAAP 2026 is understated relative to run-rate because the project losses are front-loaded; adjusted 2026 is modestly overstated relative to economic earnings because of the amortization and stock-compensation add-backs; cash 2026 is the worst of the three because of working-capital unwind. The clean test is FY2027 operating cash flow against net income.

## 7. Forward Projections And Scenario Model

### Analyst Expectations And Guidance

Company guidance for FY2026, issued November 3, 2026, is GAAP diluted EPS of $1.20–$1.35, adjusted EPS of $2.10–$2.25, net income of $65.2–$73.2 million and adjusted EBITDA of $290–$310 million, with Utilities gross margin of 10–12%, Energy gross margin of 6–8%, SG&A in the low-6% range of revenue, an effective tax rate of about 33% and interest of $43–$47 million. Management said it believes "the foundation is in place for a return to growth in 2027" but gave no FY2027 numbers.

The Street, as compiled by S&P Global on StockAnalysis as of November 10, 2026, has FY2026 revenue of $7.07 billion (range $6.8–$7.5 billion) and adjusted EPS of $2.23 (range $2.11–$2.40), and FY2027 revenue of $8.03 billion and adjusted EPS of $5.32. Sixteen analysts cover the stock: eight Strong Buy, three Buy, five Hold. The average one-year target is $100.47 (median $102, low $64, high $127). EPS and forward P/E on that page are non-GAAP adjusted.

### Estimate Revision Trend

The revision trend is steeply negative for 2026 and has barely touched 2027. FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance went from $4.80–$5.00 in May to $2.05–$2.60 in June to $2.10–$2.25 in November, a 55% cut at the midpoint. Price targets fell again after Q3: UBS from $138 to $92, Goldman Sachs from $92 to $81, Mizuho from $113 to $111, Wells Fargo from $85 to $64, and Piper Sandler from $90 to $80 (November 5–10, 2026). Yet FY2027 consensus of $5.32 is within 6% of 2025's adjusted EPS of $5.62 (S&P figure). The Street has cut the year, not the business.

That pattern is the core of the variant view. Deep cuts to the current year and shallow cuts to the next year mean analysts treat 2026 as a one-off. This report agrees that the losses are one-off but not that the volume and cost base are.

### Forecast Logic

The base case builds FY2029 adjusted EPS from the two segments. Utilities revenue grows about 6% a year from roughly $2.87 billion in 2026, supported by MSA backlog up 27% this year, at an operating margin near 8% (9M 2026: 7.3%; Q3: 9.1%). Below the operating line, interest is $52 million in 2027, a little below the Q3 2026 run-rate of about $57 million ($14.3 million in the quarter) because part of the Q3 balance is receivables-facility borrowing that working-capital release should repay, and falls to $44 million in 2029. Diluted shares fall from 54.1 million in Q3 2026 to 52.0 million in 2029 as the $250 million authorization is used, net of about 0.5 million shares a year of stock compensation. That gives FY2029 GAAP EPS of $5.02 and adjusted EPS of $5.92.

The main judgment is margin. FY2029 base GAAP operating margin of 4.8% plus about 0.5 points of amortization is roughly 5.3% before amortization, close to 2025's 5.4%. So the base case is a full return to 2025 operating profitability by 2029. On EPS it reaches the Street's FY2027 number about a year late, in FY2028, because interest and amortization are now about $1 a share higher than in 2025.

## 8. Management, Governance, And Capital Allocation

Koti Vadlamudi became President and CEO in November 2025 after 30 years at Jacobs, where he ran advanced facilities and other units. He inherited the solar projects that went wrong; the problems were disclosed within six months of his arrival. That timing makes a "kitchen sink" reading plausible: a new CEO has every incentive to recognize losses early and set a low bar. The June 22 release says the company engaged a third-party industry expert to review the projects, which is the right step. The chief operating officer, Jeremy Kinch, left on June 22, treated as a termination without cause and, per the 8-K, not related to any financial or accounting issue; the CEO is covering most COO duties while a search runs. Five months later no successor has been named in the filings captured here. For a company whose problem is project execution, a vacant COO seat is a real gap. The CFO is Ken Dodgen.

Capital allocation in 2026 has been aggressive. Primoris spent $404.7 million on PayneCrest in May, financed with $411.8 million of new debt, then bought back $50 million of stock in Q2 at about $111.29 a share and $100 million in Q3 at $75.15 (1,330,627 shares), completing its $150 million program. On October 30 the board authorized another $250 million. Buying stock at $111 two months before guiding earnings down 55% was poor timing in hindsight; buying at $75 near the trough is more defensible. The $250 million authorization is about 6.7% of the market value. The dividend is $0.08 a quarter.

The governance question to watch is whether the board holds management to the recovery. The April 30 annual meeting created a Strategy and Risk Committee chaired by Michael E. Ching; how it oversees bidding discipline on new fixed-price work is the most relevant governance variable for the next two years.

## 9. Market Debate And Variant Perception

### What Bulls Believe

Bulls believe 2026 is a one-time event confined to six legacy solar contracts signed in a boom, that the backlog proves demand, and that Utilities plus gas generation plus data-center electrical is a better business mix than the one that existed in 2025. They point to Q3: Energy gross margin of 8.5% is above the full-year 6–8% target range, Utilities posted a 13.0% gross margin, and record backlog of $14.07 billion is 2.0x trailing revenue. On that view FY2027 earnings of about $5.30 are achievable and the stock should trade at 17–20x adjusted, or $90–$105.

### What Bears Believe

Bears believe the solar business is shrinking structurally as federal tax-credit deadlines pass and developers slow new starts, that Primoris's Energy segment has excess crews and equipment it must either cut or carry, and that a contractor that just lost money on six projects will bid more conservatively and win less. They also point to the cash: $8.9 million of operating cash flow in nine months, $580.8 million of net debt after twelve months of net cash, and buybacks done at higher prices. On that view normalized EPS is closer to $4 and the right multiple is the 11–14x GAAP the stock earned before 2024.

### What The Market May Be Missing

The market may be over-reading the Q4 guide and under-reading the mix. Q4 is the seasonal low for Utilities, the last problem project finishes in Q4, and Q4 2025 revenue was already 15% below Q3 2025. A weak Q4 is consistent with a recovering business. At the same time, the Street is missing how much of the 2027 recovery depends on renewables volume that management has described as slow to start in every release this year. The Street's $8.03 billion of 2027 revenue is 13.6% growth on 2026; Q3 Energy revenue was down 17.6% year on year with PayneCrest included. Getting from minus 17.6% to plus double digits in a few quarters needs either a solar rebound or very fast conversion of new gas and data-center awards.

## 10. Bull Case

The bull case is that the business Primoris will be in 2028 is better than the one it was in 2025. Total MSA backlog grew 22% in nine months to $8.50 billion, $8.01 billion of it in Utilities, and Utilities margins are at the top of their range; utilities are spending on grid hardening and load growth for data centers. Energy's new work is gas-fired generation and data-center electrical, which tends to be larger, more negotiated and less exposed to tax-credit timing than solar. PayneCrest adds electrical skills that Primoris can sell into its existing industrial and power customers.

In the bull case revenue grows 13.5% in 2027 (to $7.95 billion, close to the Street's $8.03 billion) and 10% a year in 2028 and 2029 to $9.61 billion, GAAP operating margin rises to 5.6% by 2029 (a little above 2025's 5.4% as mix improves), the share count falls to 51.5 million, and FY2029 adjusted EPS is $7.84 ($6.92 GAAP). At 17x adjusted (about 19.3x GAAP, still below the 23–25x of 2024–2025) the stock is worth $133.20. The evidence that would point here first is a FY2027 adjusted EPS guide at or above $5.00 and Energy gross margin above 8% in Q4 2026 and Q1 2027.

## 11. Bear Case

The bear case is that Energy margins settle at the bottom of the new 6–8% range or below, renewables revenue keeps falling toward $1.5 billion as the tax-credit pull-forward ends, and gas and data-center work only replaces part of it. Revenue grows 1% a year to $7.21 billion in 2029. GAAP operating margin reaches only 3.6% (between 2026's distressed level and 2023's 4.4%), interest rises to about $60 million because cash is absorbed by working capital and debt is not repaid, buybacks stop and the share count drifts to 53.5 million. FY2029 adjusted EPS is $3.43 ($2.57 GAAP).

At 10x adjusted (about 13.3x GAAP, near the top of the stock's 2018–2023 range of 8.9–14.3x year-end trailing P/E, whose median is 12.8x) the stock is worth $34.26, 52% below $71.24. The mechanism is not a collapse; it is Primoris reverting to the mid-margin, mid-multiple contractor it was before 2024. The early evidence would be a FY2027 guide below $4.00, any further loss provision on renewables, or Energy fixed backlog shrinking as projects burn faster than they are replaced.

## 12. Base Case

The most likely outcome is a slow recovery. Q4 2026 lands near the bottom half of the guide, the last challenged project completes, and management guides 2027 to roughly $4.50–$5.00 of adjusted EPS with revenue up high single digits. Utilities keeps growing mid-single digits at 11–13% gross margin. Energy gross margin climbs from the 6–8% range toward 9% by 2028 as underused crews move to gas and electrical work and new solar is bid at wider margins. Buybacks absorb about 2 million shares over three years.

That path produces FY2027 adjusted EPS of $4.59, FY2028 of $5.28 and FY2029 of $5.92 ($5.02 GAAP). At 13x adjusted, or about 15.3x GAAP, the stock is worth $76.96 in three years, a 2.6% annual return from $71.24 before dividends. That multiple sits one point above the top of the stock's 2018–2023 range (14.3x GAAP) because the mix is now more utility MSA and power work than in the pipeline-heavy years, and below the 2024–2025 rerating because the market has learned that the Energy segment can lose a year.

## 15. Risks And Red Flags

The biggest risk is that the Energy segment's margin problem is not six projects but a cost structure built for $3 billion of solar that now has $2 billion. If so, every quarter carries idle crews until management cuts them, and cutting skilled crews in a tight labor market is costly to reverse. That is the bear case, and it is the reason for a 30% weight.

The second risk is the balance sheet working-capital position. Receivables and contract assets of $1.82 billion against $7.06 billion of trailing revenue mean a write-down of claims on the troubled projects would hit both earnings and cash. Net debt of $580.8 million is about 2.0x the low end of the 2026 adjusted EBITDA guide ($290 million) and would pass 2.5x only if adjusted EBITDA fell below about $230 million.

Other risks: management credibility after three cuts; a COO vacancy during the recovery; fixed-price bidding on new gas and data-center projects, which are larger than solar jobs; utility customers delaying MSA releases if rates or interest costs pressure their capital budgets; and the higher amortization and interest from PayneCrest leaving GAAP EPS about $1.00 a share below adjusted EPS in the 2027 base case.

## 19. Conclusion

Primoris is a Watch. The 2026 losses look contained to six solar projects that are nearly finished, and the Utilities half of the business is in its best shape in years with a record backlog behind it. But $71.24 already pays about 13.4x the Street's FY2027 recovery number, and this report expects that recovery to take until 2028–2029 because solar volume is shrinking, the Energy cost base has to be resized, and interest has roughly doubled. The probability-weighted value is $75.40, about 1.9% a year, with a base case of $76.96, a bear case of $34.26 and a bull case of $133.20.

The stock works if FY2027 guidance shows Energy margins back above 8% and earnings near $5 a share; it fails if the solar business keeps shrinking faster than gas and data-center work replaces it. I would revisit the view at the Q4 2026 release around February 22, 2027, and would turn more constructive on a guide at or above $5.00, and would rate the stock an Own on that evidence only in the mid-$50s.
