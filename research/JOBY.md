## 1. Thesis In One Page

### The Setup

Joby Aviation is the most capitalized and best-known public company building an electric vertical takeoff and landing aircraft for urban air mobility. At $7.35 billion market cap with $2.47 billion in cash and no revenue-generating product, the company represents one of the largest bets in the public markets on the UAM industry's commercial viability. The stock has fallen 57% from its 52-week high of $20.95, compressing from a $20.7 billion peak to near the $6.89 bottom. The short interest has climbed to 15.21% of float, and analyst estimates remain deeply negative through FY27.

The real question is not whether Joby can build a functional eVTOL aircraft — the aircraft is already flying and has accumulated thousands of flight hours. The question is whether the FAA Type Certificate timeline, capital burn rate, and expected dilution combine to produce per-share value creation, or whether Joby becomes a well-funded technology incubator that returns nothing to common shareholders after 3-4 rounds of dilutive financing.

For the thesis to work: FAA certification must occur on schedule (~2028-2029), the $2.47B cash pile must suffice through certification without more than ~40% total dilution, and the Uber/Toyota/DHL partnership commitments must be sufficient to generate revenue that offsets operating burn before the cash runs out.

For the thesis to fail: FAA certification is delayed past mid-2030, the burn rate remains $500M+/year, and the company requires dilutive raises at below-current prices, destroying per-share value even if the product eventually succeeds. Joby at its current price has $4.96 per share of optionality premium above the cash NAV floor of $2.51/share — the market is underwriting a success case that still seems unproven.

### The Core Debate

The debate is not whether eVTOL has commercial potential. Even skeptics acknowledge that regulated air-taxi services around major metros will exist — the question is whether Joby is the platform or whether it becomes the Blackwings or Beta Technologies of the sector: well-funded, technically interesting, but either acquired or slowly diluted into irrelevancy.

At $7.35 billion with $2.47B cash, the market is paying $4.88 billion for the option that certification succeeds and commercial revenue materializes. If FAA certification arrives in 2028-2029 as management projects, the option has value. If certification slips to 2030+, the cash burn through that period requires additional dilution that eats through the premium.

The stock's trajectory since the 52-week high of $20.95 tells part of the story. Even at that peak, Joby was paying 266x trailing revenue. At current levels, it's still 95x trailing and 65x forward, and revenue growth is not yet proven. The 93% QoQ revenue decline in the latest quarter is the most informative data point: it shows that Joby has no stable revenue base and that "growth" in these financials is project-specific, not market-driven.

The core underwriting conflict is between the size of the claimed market ($1T+ for urban air mobility by 2040 in industry projections) and the capital requirements to get there. Joby needs to survive a 2-4 year certification wait with a $500M+/year burn rate, which means either the cash pile suffices or shareholders get diluted. There is no middle ground.

## 2. Company Story

Joby Aviation was founded in 2009 by JoeBen Bevirt, a former aerospace engineer who worked at Lockheed Martin, BAE Systems, and other defense contractors. The company's origins lie in Bevirt's conviction that urban air mobility — short-distance, on-demand electric flights within and around cities — represented the most significant transportation innovation since the automobile. The company was named "Joby" after a 1980s cartoon character (Joby the pterodactyl from the movie *The Secret of NIMH*), reflecting Bevirt's early fascination with personal air travel.

Joby gained prominence in 2018-2019 through a series of strategic partnerships and capital raises. Uber's launch of its Uber Elevate urban air mobility platform in 2019 made Joby the designated aircraft manufacturer for what would become the Uber Air service. Toyota became a strategic investor and manufacturing partner in 2019, a deal that sent the company's valuation through the roof and signaled automotive-industry-scale engineering resources. DHL partnered for cargo operations in 2020. Each partnership served as both a technical validation and a capital catalyst.

The SPAC debut occurred in the late-2020/early-2021 wave that saw a flurry of eVTOL and climate-tech go-public events. Joby's de-SPAC was significant in raising over $1.2 billion in combined PIPE and trust capital — making it one of the largest pure-play eVTOL companies listed publicly. At that stage, the aircraft was in its S-94 configuration, and test flights were ongoing.

Since going public, Joby has iterated the aircraft platform (currently referred to as the S4/S5 series depending on the configuration), maintained its FAA interaction through the certification process, and built manufacturing capacity with Toyota in Long Beach, California. The Long Beach facility represents Joby's transition from prototyping to serial manufacturing, and it accounts for the dramatic increase in capital expenditures. A $1 billion manufacturing facility investment is the single largest infrastructure commitment in the eVTOL sector.

Joby now sits as the most capitalized public eVTOL platform by market value. It has the advantage of brand recognition, partner diversity (mobility platform via Uber, manufacturing via Toyota, cargo via DHL), and a demonstrated flight program with thousands of hours logged. It also faces the structural challenge of every pre-commercial technology company: surviving the gap between current cash burn and future revenue generation without destroying per-share value.

## 3. Business Model

Joby's revenue model, in theory, is a combination of aircraft sales, air-taxi service revenue through partnerships, and cargo operations. In practice, as of Q1 2026, all revenue is project-based, research-related, or contract work that does not represent recurring commercial operations. The TTM revenue of $77.7 million grew 45% YoY to $53.4M in FY2025 from $136K in FY2024 — a dramatic increase that masks the fragility of its sources.

The revenue spike from near-zero in early 2024 to $53.4M in FY2025 and $77.7M TTM is driven by specific government contracts and research programs, not organic market demand. The 93% QoQ decline in the most recent quarter proves this is not stable revenue. The 13% QoQ decline in Q1 2026 (per Yahoo Scout's financial summary) shows the revenue base remains project-dependent. Analysts estimate $113.9M revenue in FY2026 (up 113% from FY2025) and $221.1M in FY2027 (up 94%), but these are consensus averages from 8-9 analysts and reflect expectations around contract renewals and government program participation, not organic product-market fit.

The economic engine is the aircraft-as-a-service model. The end game involves Joby manufacturing eVTOL aircraft and operating them through Uber's mobility platform for air-taxi services, with DHL handling cargo operations. The per-seat cost target of $14.99 for urban air taxi is Joby's headline metric — it represents the break-even price below which air-taxi cannot compete with existing ground transportation. At this price point, the margin structure depends on aircraft utilization, battery lifecycle, maintenance costs, and pilot compensation.

Unit economics depend on the vehicle reaching production scale with the Long Beach facility. Toyota's involvement is designed to bring automotive-scale manufacturing efficiency to what is essentially an aerospace product. The challenge is that aviation manufacturing is fundamentally different from auto manufacturing — certification requirements, safety margins, and supply chain complexity are orders of magnitude higher. Joby needs to produce aircraft at a cost that allows the $14.99/seat target while absorbing battery degradation, maintenance, insurance, and pilot costs.

Joby's gross margin on the $77.7M TTM revenue is approximately 38% ($29.5M gross profit). This is decent for the revenue mix it generates (government contracts, research programs), but it tells us nothing about the economics of commercial air-taxi operations. The gross margin on air-taxi flights will be fundamentally different from the gross margin on a consulting or research contract.

Operating expenses of $819.4M TTM dwarf revenue. Joby is an engineering company in the early development phase, and the operating expense profile reflects that: engineering, R&D, manufacturing setup, regulatory interaction, and corporate infrastructure. The operating loss of $789.9M TTM is a structural feature, not a transient condition.

The company is essentially burning through its $2.47B war chest to fund the gap between today and commercial operations. At $543M TTM operating cash flow burn, the runway is approximately 4.5 years. If the burn rate declines as revenue increases and manufacturing ramps, the runway extends. If the burn rate accelerates (as the QoQ data suggests), the runway shortens and dilution becomes inevitable.

## 4. Products, Segments, And Moat

Joby's primary product is the Joby S4/S5 eVTOL aircraft — a four-passenger (three passengers + pilot) electric aircraft designed for urban and sub-urban air travel. The aircraft has a range of approximately 150 miles per charge, a top speed of about 200 mph, and is designed for vertical takeoff and landing without requiring traditional runway infrastructure. The aircraft uses eight rotors and a hybrid configuration optimized for efficiency at cruise altitude while maintaining vertical-lift capability.

The production platform is undergoing serial manufacturing at the Long Beach, California facility built in partnership with Toyota. The Long Beach facility was designed for production scale — the investment implies a target of hundreds to thousands of aircraft eventually, not the dozens that Joby was building during the prototype phase.

Joby currently has no operating segments in the conventional sense. Revenue is not disaggregated because all revenue comes from the same basket of government contracts, research work, and program participation. There is no meaningful "air-taxi segment," "cargo segment," or "aircraft sales segment" to analyze independently.

The competitive position in the eVTOL sector is characterized by several active players. Archer Aviation (ACHR, ~$3.5B MC) is perhaps the closest competitor, also FAA-certification-seeking and also partnership-funded (Ford, Volocopter adjacent). The key advantage Joby has over Archer is Toyota's manufacturing backing and the Uber platform integration. Archer has a simpler product architecture (smaller, fewer seats) which could lead to faster certification timelines but narrower operational envelope.

Other competitors include EHang (EH), a Chinese eVTOL operator that is actually further along commercially in Asian markets (it has received Chinese CAAC certification for its EH216-S and is operating trials in China); Volocopter (VOLT.DE), a German company with a different technology architecture and Dubai/Paris focus; and Beta Technologies (BEET), which has received some FAA progress and focuses on cargo. The competitive advantage for Joby comes from scale of capital, partner quality, and the U.S. first-mover positioning.

The moat assessment is moderate. Joby's moat comes from three sources: (1) the regulatory interaction history — the FAA relationship and the certification roadmap itself is a competitive advantage because the first certified aircraft gets first mover advantage in a highly regulated market; (2) partner network — Uber provides the demand-side platform, Toyota provides supply-side manufacturing scale, and DHL provides cargo operations; (3) flight hours — accumulated test flight data that feeds directly into the certification process. None of these moats is deep: competitors can enter FAA certification, partners have alternative arrangements, and flight hours are a sunk cost that competitors with similar capitalization can match.

The moat is not the technology. eVTOL technology is not proprietary in the semiconductor or biotechnology sense — multiple companies have demonstrated functional aircraft. The moat is the combination of capital depth (Joby has $2.47B cash, the most among public eVTOL companies), partner quality (Toyota/Uber), and regulatory positioning (the FAA relationship and certification roadmap stage). This moat is defensible but not insurmountable.

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

Joby's earnings quality is **noisy** — and more accurately, earnings are completely irrelevant as a quality metric because the company has no earnings. The net loss of $957.4M TTM is composed of operational costs (R&D, engineering, manufacturing buildout, corporate infrastructure) plus non-cash items (stock-based compensation) and "other income/expense" that can mask one-time mechanics.

The most informative quality metric is the operating cash flow vs. net income divergence. TTM operating cash flow is -$543.4M vs. net income of -$957.4M — a gap of $414M. The gap exists because: (1) stock-based compensation is a major non-cash expense; (2) depreciation and amortization of the Long Beach facility and capitalized costs flow through net income but not cash flow; and (3) the "other income/expense" line of -$217.1M TTM (per income statement data) includes non-cash and potentially non-recurring items.

The quality assessment is mixed. On the positive side, the cash flow burn ($543M TTM) is less than the net loss ($957M), indicating that significant portions of the loss are non-cash. The depreciation line of $42.0M TTM is relatively modest for a manufacturing facility that was invested heavily in 2025. The non-cash charges are consistent with the accounting treatment of a pre-commercial developer.

On the negative side, the "other expense" line of -$217.1M TTM is large and concerning. In prior years this was positive ($117.5M in FY2022, $45.6M FY2023), suggesting one-time mechanics that flip sign. Normalized income (per Yahoo Finance calculations) is -$827.1M vs. reported -$957.4M, meaning the unusual items add $130M of loss beyond the operating trajectory. This makes FY2025 earnings quality noisy — the unusual items include both positive and negative swings that are not reflective of the business.

The interest income of $51.05M TTM is a legitimate and recurring cash inflow — it's not earnings management. It's the yield on $2.47B deployed in money market/T-bills. This is a genuine operating cash inflow.

The bottom line on quality: Joby does not produce meaningful earnings. The quality framework is inappropriate here because the company is structurally loss-making by design. The relevant quality question is: is the cash burn trajectory reliable for runway estimation? The answer is yes — operating cash flow is a relatively clean signal because the major non-cash items (SBC, depreciation) are well-documented, and the other income/expense line is the primary noise source.

**Earnings quality rating**: Not applicable — pre-commercial, structurally loss-making. Cash burn reliability: moderate with caveats on "other expense" volatility.

## 9. Market Debate And Variant Perception

The market debate around JOBY centers on three axes: timeline credibility, capital efficiency, and competitive positioning.

The bulls' argument is that Joby is the best-positioned eVTOL company in the public markets. It has the most cash ($2.47B), the strongest partner network (Toyota/Uber/DHL), the most advanced certification roadmap, and the most tangible manufacturing capacity. Bulls see the $20.95 52-week high as overpriced but the current $7.47 as a discount to the option value of the certification binary. At the low end, the $2.51/share cash NAV floor means downside is limited. The bull case is that certification arrives in 2028-2029, the runway suffices, and Joby becomes the dominant UAM platform in U.S. markets alongside Uber integration. The 113% revenue growth expectation for FY2026 and 94% for FY2027 feed into this scenario.

The bears' argument is that eVTOL is a decade away from meaningful commercial scale, Joby's burn rate makes runway unsustainable, and every certification cycle produces delays that compound. At $500M+/year burn with $2.47B cash, the runway is a maximum of 4.5 years, and 4.5 years is tight for FAA certification + first commercial operations. Bears also point to the dilution history (58% share increase since FY2022), the QoQ revenue collapse (93% decline in one quarter), and the competitive landscape (Archer, EHang, Volocopter, Beta each with viable claims). The 15.21% short interest reflects institutional skepticism on timeline. The bear case is that Joby becomes a well-funded technology incubator that returns $0.50-$2.00 per share after certification + dilution, barely above the cash NAV floor.

What the market is missing, in my assessment, is the partner commitment question. The Uber, Toyota, and DHL partnerships frame Joby as having market access pre-certification. However, MOUs and partnership announcements are not binding revenue commitments. If any of these partners pivot to competing platforms during the certification wait, the option value of Joby's revenue pipeline vanishes. Joby needs these partnerships to be real post-certification, not just pre-certification credibility. The test will come when certification is imminent and partners have to decide whether to commit capital and operations.

The variant perception opportunity is in the timing of the catalyst. Current pricing ($7.47) encodes a roughly 27% success probability for the full bull case. If FAA certification advances faster than expected (e.g., partial certification, operational waiver granted 12-18 months earlier than 2028), the optionality premium re-rates upward. ## 11. Bear Case

The bear case for JOBY is a scenario where the certification timeline slips, the burn rate outpaces the runway, and dilution erodes per-share value to the point where the optionality premium is destroyed. Here's the mechanism:

The certification delay is the primary failure mode. The FAA Type Certificate process for an entirely new class of aircraft (eVTOL) takes longer than the 2028-2029 timeline — perhaps to 2030-2031. This can be caused by safety incidents during flight testing, regulatory conservatism in a high-visibility public company, supply chain disruptions in the certification process, or simply the complexity of a new aircraft class that lacks comparable regulatory precedent. The certification cycle itself is the single biggest risk — all other risks flow from whether the aircraft gets certified on schedule.

At $700M+/year burn rate (the bear case assumption, reflecting potential cost escalation as the company adds infrastructure before revenue materializes), the $2.47B cash pile provides only 3.5 years of runway. When these contracts end, they don't renew automatically. The $113.9M FY2026 analyst consensus is based on the assumption that contracts continue — if they don't, revenue could remain flat or even contract. Without commercial operations, there is no revenue trajectory that offsets the burn.

Partnerships deteriorate during the certification wait. Uber evaluates alternative platforms (Archer has a potentially faster certification path with its simpler architecture). Toyota considers whether the manufacturing investment has returned adequate value. DHL explores other cargo options. The partnerships were valuable for credibility and capital access, but they are not binding revenue commitments — the bear case is that the partner network dissolves during a multi-year certification delay.

The diluted share value at $0.50 per share in the model is essentially the residual option after cash depletion, dilution, and failed certification timeline. Even in this worst case, the company is not bankrupt — the $2.47B cash provides a floor, the manufacturing facility has residual value, and the aircraft platform has inherent technology value. But the per-share value has been destroyed by dilution.

Required assumptions: (1) FAA certification delayed past 2030, (2) accelerated burn rate to $700M+/year, (3) 2x dilution from capital raises at depressed prices, (4) project revenue fails to renew and commercial revenue doesn't materialize, (5) partner commitments weaken during the delay period.
