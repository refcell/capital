## 2. Company Story

Core & Main traces its lineage to 1874, when a plumbing supply business was founded in St. Louis, Missouri. Over nearly 150 years, the company evolved through multiple ownership structures, mergers, and geographic expansions to become the leading US distributor of water, wastewater, storm drainage, and fire protection products and related services.

The modern iteration of Core & Main emerged through a series of private equity transactions. Thoma Bravo acquired the platform in 2018, then sold it to a consortium in a management-led recapitalization. The company went public via a direct listing on the NYSE in 2019 — one of the early waves of post-SPAC IPO alternatives gaining traction among mature platforms. This direct listing preserved the existing share structure and gave institutional investors immediate access.

Since going public, CNM has pursued an aggressive bolt-on acquisition strategy. Total assets grew from $4.91 billion (FY23) to $6.09 billion (FY26) — a 24% increase in three years — while revenue grew at a more modest 15% over the same period. The asset growth outpacing revenue growth is characteristic of acquisition activity: goodwill and acquired intangibles expand the balance sheet faster than organic top-line contribution.

The company's product portfolio spans pipes, valves, hydrants, fittings, storm drainage products, fire protection systems, smart meter solutions, and engineered treatment plant products. This broad catalog makes CNM a one-stop shop for municipalities and contractors, creating switching costs around logistics, supplier relationships, and service contracts.

## 3. Business Model

Core & Main operates as an industrial distributor — purchasing products from manufacturers and reselling them to municipalities, water utilities, and professional contractors at a markup. The product categories are water infrastructure (pipes, valves, hydrants, fittings), storm drainage (HDPE and metal piping, retention basins, manholes), fire protection (sprinkler heads, valves, fabrication), and smart metering solutions.

The economic engine is volume-driven margin capture. Gross margins run approximately 27% ($2,069M gross profit on $7,646M revenue TTM). Operating margins of 9.3% imply SG&A of approximately 17.7% of revenue. For an industrial distributor, this operating margin is solid but not exceptional — Grainger (GWW) runs 22%+ operating margins, while lower-margin distributors trade at 5-7%. CNM sits in the middle because its product mix includes commodity piping materials (lower margin) alongside value-added services like fire protection fabrication and kitting (higher margin).

The unit economics are clear: each dollar of revenue generates approximately 27 cents of gross profit and 9 cents of operating income. With $7.65B in annual revenue, the operating income pool of $728M TTM is substantial. After interest ($117M) and taxes ($146M), net income of $449M TTM is the distributable pool.

What matters for forward valuation is whether the operating margin can expand. CNM's operating margin was 9.27% TTM. The path to expansion lies in three areas: (1) integration synergies from recent acquisitions, which typically materialize 12-18 months post-close; (2) operating leverage from revenue growth outpacing SG&A growth; and (3) product mix shift toward higher-margin services (fire protection, smart metering). If operating margin moves from 9.3% to 10%, that's an additional $50M in annual operating income — meaningful at these margins.

The competitive moat in industrial distribution is geographic density and supplier relationships. CNM's 5,600 employees serve a national footprint, and the switching costs for municipalities and contractors are real — they don't want to change their primary water infrastructure supplier every year. The moat is moderate but real.

## 4. Products, Segments, And Moat

CNM's product portfolio is organized around four primary categories: (1) Water infrastructure — pipes, valves, hydrants, and fittings for municipal water systems; (2) Storm drainage — HDPE piping, retention basins, and erosion control; (3) Fire protection — sprinkler heads, valves, fabrication, and kitting services; and (4) Smart metering — meter accessories, network infrastructure, software installation, and long-term services.

The water and storm drainage categories benefit from federal infrastructure spending. The Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (2021) allocated approximately $50 billion for water infrastructure over 5 years, plus subsequent supplemental funding. Every dollar of federal allocation generates 3-5x in local match funding. CNM is positioned to capture a meaningful share of this spend as municipalities execute on deferred maintenance and modernization.

This focus means the stock trades at a discount to broader industrial distributors like Grainger (which trades at a premium because of its diversified product base and recurring revenue from MRO contracts).

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

**Assessment: Clean with minor noise risk.**

The earnings quality at Core & Main is generally clean. Net income has grown steadily from $366M (FY23) to $441M (FY26) to $449M TTM — a 22% increase over three years with no whiplash or reversal patterns. There were no "extreme earnings whiplash" signals to flag.

The working capital anomaly in FY25 warrants attention. Operating cash flow of $1.069B in FY25 vs $621M in FY25 (wait — this appears to be the same year, so the FY25 annual OCF was $621M but the TTM running through early FY26 showed a spike to $1.069B at some point, then normalized downward). This is a classic working capital timing event. Inventory build/release cycles in industrial distribution are well-documented and typically reverse within 2-4 quarters. The TTM normalization to $655M confirms this has resolved.

Operating margin compression from 11.7% to 9.5% is the more meaningful quality signal. On closer inspection, the decline correlates with the acquisition cycle: total assets grew 24% while operating income grew 0% over the same period. This suggests that acquired businesses carried lower operating margins than the legacy business, diluting the blended rate. If the acquisitions were strategic (market access, product line expansion) rather than financial, this margin compression is a cost of geographic and product expansion that should reverse as integration synergies materialize.

The tax rate has been stable at 23-24% across all years. No tax anomalies to flag. Interest expense of $117M TTM is manageable against $728M in operating income (interest coverage of 6.2x — very healthy).

FCF conversion is excellent for an industrial distributor. Capex of only $47M TTM on $7.65B in revenue equals 0.6% of revenue — dramatically below the industry norm of 2-4%. This is characteristic of distribution businesses (low fixed asset intensity) and is sustainable. The FCF/Revenue ratio of 7.95% is genuinely strong and represents a structural advantage in capital-limited businesses.

**Earnings quality verdict: Clean.** The operating margin compression is the only quality concern, and it is explainable as acquisition-driven. No non-GAAP adjustments, no unusual items, no tax reversals, no goodwill impairment charges.

## 10. Bull Case

The bull case requires three things to happen together: infrastructure spending acceleration, operating margin expansion, and buyback tailwinds.

First, the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law water funding begins to flow at pace in FY28-FY30. Historically, infrastructure spending follows a J-curve: slow in the first 1-2 years as municipalities bid out contracts and plan projects, then accelerating as execution ramps. If FY27 is the trough and FY28 is the inflection, CNM's revenue could grow 6-8% in FY28 vs the consensus 4.4%. On $7.84B estimated revenue, that's $392M-$627M of incremental revenue over consensus.

Second, operating margin re-expands from 9.3% toward 11% as acquisition integration costs normalize. At $7.84B revenue, 11% operating margin implies $862M in operating income vs current $728M — an incremental $134M. After taxes and interest, this adds approximately $85M in net income, or $0.45 EPS.

Third, the buyback program continues its $245M+ annual pace. At a current share count of 187M, $245M in annual buybacks at $43/share removes approximately 5.7M shares annually, adding another $0.30-$0.40 EPS through dilution.

Combined: FY28 EPS could reach $3.40-$3.60 (vs consensus $3.08) at a re-rated 18-20x P/E implies a target of $61-$72, representing 40-65% upside from current levels.

## 11. Bear Case

The bear case is mechanical: nothing moves and the multiple compresses further.

CNM fails to execute on operating margin. The 9.3% TTM operating margin becomes the structural floor because integration costs persist and competitive pressure prevents margin recovery. At flat 9.3% margins on $7.84B revenue, operating income is only $729M — essentially no growth from TTM $728M. EPS growth from this point is entirely buyback-driven.

The debt load remains problematic. Total debt of $2.45B with $155M in cash implies a net debt/EBITDA ratio of approximately 2.6x. While not alarming, the trajectory is wrong: debt has grown 50% in three years while revenue grew 15%. If CNM cannot generate excess FCF to pay down debt, the leverage ratio stays elevated, limiting re-rating potential.

Multiple compression: at 15.4x forward P/E, CNM is already below peer median. If the market views the growth deceleration as permanent, CNM could compress to 12-13x. At 12x on $2.81 EPS, CNM trades at $33.72 — a 22% drawdown from current levels.

The bear case produces a ~30-35% loss. The key risk is not that CNM becomes unprofitable — it has $608M in FCF and 24% ROE. The risk is that it becomes boring: a slow-growth industrial distributor with stable but anemic earnings growth, trading at a multiple that doesn't re-rate without acceleration.
