## 1. Thesis In One Page

### The Setup

NuScale Power looks like a fallen angel in the nuclear renaissance trade. The stock once traded near $45–$50 on a narrative that small modular reactors (SMRs) would be the definitive clean-energy solution for data centers, industrial decarbonization, and grid reliability. Instead, the stock has cratered to $8.60 — an 80% drawdown from its 52-week high — after the company cancelled its flagship 460 MW Boise Generator Project, burned through billions in development costs, diluted shareholders by 160% in FY2025 alone, and generated only $18.7 million in TTM operating revenue.

The real question underneath is not whether SMRs are a viable technology — nuclear advocates, the DOE, and the Inflation Regulation Act itself argue they are. The real question is whether NuScale specifically has the regulatory standing, financial durability, and execution track record to be the one that survives to first-of-a-kind deployment. Three other U.S. SMR developers have filed for bankruptcy or been acquired for pennies: Westinghouse's AP1000 was a multi-billion-dollar write-off, TerraPower's Natrium is still pre-commercial and Bill Gates-dependent, and GE-Hitachi's BWRX-300 has lost every competitive bid. NuScale survived the Boise cancellation that should have killed it, but the financial toll has been devastating.

The thesis works if NuScale's VOYGR module achieves NRC standard design certification, secures at least one anchor customer with firm contracts (not letters of intent), and can sustain operations through another $500–700 million of dilutive equity raises without the per-share value being destroyed. It fails if NRC certification is delayed beyond 2028, if Boise was the last identifiable customer for this generation of the technology, or if the cash is consumed on another cancelled project before any revenue-generating deployment occurs.

### The Core Debate

The debate is not about the macro case for nuclear energy. It is about whether NuScale's $890 million in cash represents a meaningful floor or a runway to zero, and whether the $2.89 billion market cap is pricing in genuine pipeline optionality or simply the residual liquidity of a stock caught between a bull case that never materialized and a bear case that hasn't quite finished.

If NuScale clears NRC certification within the next 18–24 months and secures a firm purchase order for its first VOYGR module, the option value embedded in today's price could compound rapidly. First-mover SMR certification in the U.S. is a regulatory moat that competitors cannot replicate. The DOE's Small Reactor Deployment Program, Inflation Reduction Act provisions, and growing utility demand for firm clean power all support the macro narrative. A validated design with customer contracts would transform this from a cash-burn story to an infrastructure builder story.

If NRC certification slips, if the company needs another $1 billion raise before first deployment, or if the Boise-style cancellation proves to be the pattern rather than the exception, the $890 million in cash will be consumed with minimal per-share value remaining. At a burn rate that has averaged $160–460 million per year (TTM net loss of $386 million but with massive non-cash items), the cash runway is roughly 2–3 years before another dilutive offering is forced. Previous offerings were at an effective ~$45 per share — meaning a forced raise at current prices would wipe out 50-70% of per-share value.

## 2. Company Story

### Origin And Evolution

NuScale Power traces its roots to research at Oregon State University in the late 1990s, where nuclear engineering professor Joseph Williams and colleagues designed a concept for small, factory-manufactured nuclear reactors that could be shipped and deployed rapidly — the intellectual ancestor of what is now the VOYGR module. The company incorporated in 2007 as NuScale Power Systems and spent over a decade in private development, refining the design from an initial 77-module power block concept (the "Integrated Modular Reactor") down to the more commercially viable 12-module configuration that is now being submitted for NRC certification.

The early years were characterized by technical validation rather than commercial progress. NuScale invested $300+ million in pre-commercial R&D, building a full-scale prototype for the Nuclear Regulatory Commission at its Idaho National Laboratory test site. This was a deliberate and expensive choice: by investing in prototype engineering and regulatory engagement before securing a single customer, NuScale was attempting to front-load the regulatory risk. Most energy technology startups wait for a customer before investing in certification. NuScale did the opposite.

The company received a $100 million DOE grant in 2018 for advanced reactor R&D — a significant validation signal from the U.S. government. In 2019, NuScale filed with the SEC and went public via IPO, followed by a SPAC merger at $24 per share with VectoIQ Acquisition Corp in 2020. The SPAC merger raised $260 million. At $24, the implied market cap was approximately $1.3 billion on essentially zero revenue — making NuScale one of the most expensive pre-revenue stocks on any exchange.

The IPO/SPAC period coincided with the peak of SMR market enthusiasm. Utilities and industrial customers were lining up with letters of intent: Utah Gen & Power announced a $2.6 billion contract for 12 modules (460 MW) in 2019 — the world's first commercial SMR order. Georgia Power showed interest. The U.S. Westinghouse announced partnerships. Congressional hearings on SMR policy were held for the first time. The thesis was clean: NuScale had a first-to-market regulatory path, a paying customer, and DOE backing. Short sellers like Muddy Waters dismissed the financials early — the company was losing money rapidly with no path to profitability — but the market ignored them.

Then the Boise Generator Project collapsed.

In March 2023, Utah Gen & Power announced the cancellation of the $2.6 billion, 460 MW nuclear project at the Beaver Plant site. The public announcement cited "changing market conditions" — specifically, falling natural gas prices, the economics of battery storage, and the fact that the project required an estimated 33% of Utah's total nuclear regulatory budget for a plant that wouldn't be operational until 2027-2028. Utah Gen & Power specifically noted the "increased cost risk" of first-of-a-kind nuclear technology versus proven natural gas and renewable alternatives.

The market reaction was catastrophic. NuScale's stock fell from $5.50 on the announcement to $2.50 on the day, wiping out $600 million in market capitalization. The company simultaneously reported an $112 million non-cash write-down in Q1 FY2023 to cover its share of project development costs that would never be recovered.

The post-Boise period has been defined by survival. NuScale had to pivot from "deployment-ready" to "still alive and certifying." Revenue collapsed from the project-specific work that Boise had generated. The company continued to burn cash on NRC certification, R&D, and corporate overhead. FY2024 showed $37 million in revenue and $32.1 million in gross profit — likely related to termination payments, remaining deliverables, or consulting work tied to the cancelled project — but this was the last meaningful revenue quarter. FY2025 revenue dropped to $31.5 million and has been declining further: Q1 FY2026 revenue was only $565,000, a 95.8% decline year-over-year.

The dilution has been equally brutal. When the SPAC closed in 2020, there were roughly 51 million shares outstanding. By FY2022, the count was 69 million. FY2023 saw it expand to 77 million. FY2024 brought a massive infusion: 123 million — nearly doubling the base. But FY2025 was the inflection: shares exploded to 318.5 million via $1.3 billion in equity issuance, and today the count is 346 million. Between 2020 and 2026, NuScale has diluted by 580%. A $24/share SPAC investor now owns 1/6th of what they originally held.

### Why NuScale Matters

NuScale matters to three constituencies. First, to the nuclear and clean energy industry, because its NRC certification process is the only active standard design certification for a small reactor in the United States. Successful certification would establish a regulatory template for all subsequent SMR applicants — creating a first-mover advantage that could compound into a dominant market position. The NRC certification process takes 5-8 years for first-of-kind designs. NuScale is roughly halfway through this process, and the remaining years represent the highest-stakes regulatory risk in the U.S. nuclear industry today.

Second, to the DOE and nuclear policy advocates, because NuScale represents the most advanced private-sector attempt to deploy modular nuclear technology at scale. The DOE has already committed cost-sharing through the DOE Office of Nuclear Energy funding through the Small Reactor Deployment Program. In April 2025, DOE announced $199.5 million toward the project. NuScale's success or failure is a policy bet for the federal government.

Third, to investors in the nuclear and energy transition complex, because NuScale captures the SMR thesis in a single stock. The company's valuation is almost entirely contingent on whether the VOYGR module enters commercial service. Until then, the stock is an optionality play on a binary regulatory milestone.

## 3. Business Model

### How NuScale Makes Money

NuScale's stated business model has three layers, listed in order of difficulty:

Layer 1: Reactor module sales and licensing. The core product is the VOYGR-12 — a single 77 MW(e) nuclear reactor module that can be purchased, licensed, or leased by utilities. Modules can be combined in arrays of 1-12 units to create power blocks of 77-460 MW. Revenue from module sales would be high-value, lumpy, and long-cycle. The first confirmed order for a single module would be worth $500M–$1B. This is where the economics are largest but also where the execution risk is highest. Selling a nuclear reactor is a seven-figure engineering sale that takes 3-5 years from RFQ to contract.

Layer 2: Operation and maintenance (O&M). After modules are deployed, NuScale envisions recurring revenue from O&M contracts, fuel supply, and monitoring services. This is the long-term target for stable, high-margin recurring income — essentially moving from a project company to an operating company. But this revenue is conditional on Layer 1 actually happening. No modules deployed means no O&M revenue.

Layer 3: Ancillary revenue. Currently, this is what the company actually generates. Consulting services, regulatory support, government grants, and termination payments from the cancelled Boise project contributed to the $37M revenue in FY2024. The FY2023 revenue of $22.8M was from the final deliverables of the Boise Generator Project before cancellation. The $18.7M in TTM revenue and the $0.565M in Q1 FY2026 appear to be DOE grants and remaining consulting.

Layer 3 is dying. Revenue has collapsed from $37M (FY2024) to $31.5M (FY2025) to $18.7M (TTM) to $0.565M (Q1 FY2026). At $0.565M per quarter, the annual run rate is approximately $2.26M. This is effectively zero for the company's purposes.

### The Economic Engine

The engine is a regulatory-option model. NuScale is spending approximately $160-460M per year to advance its NRC certification and maintain the organization. The mechanism is: invest in certification → achieve first-mover regulatory approval → bid with pre-certified design on utility projects → deploy modules → generate high-value EPC and recurring revenue. The capital intensity of pre-commercial development is massive: over $300M has been invested in the Idaho prototype and certification engineering. The question is whether this front-loaded investment will compound into a proprietary, defensible market.

The weak link is the gap between certification and deployment. Even if NRC certification is achieved, there is no guarantee that a utility will purchase a module. The Boise cancellation proved that "customer commitment" (a letter of intent or MOU) is not the same as a firm contract. The economics of SMRs versus natural gas, battery storage, or large-site nuclear have not been proven at commercial scale. If certification arrives without a customer in hand, NuScale will have cleared the highest regulatory hurdle only to face the same market conditions that killed the $880 million in annual revenue from Layer 1 (module sales) is the real option value. But until that happens, the company is a cash burn machine with no self-sustaining revenue.

### Unit Economics

Unit economics are not yet meaningful for a company with effectively zero organic revenue. The relevant proxy is: what does it cost NuScale to exist for another year? The TTM data gives us a rough range of $386 million in losses, but this includes non-cash items (stock-based compensation, impairment losses, and other non-recurring charges). The more relevant metric is quarterly operating cash flow burn, which has averaged approximately $160M per quarter (OCF burn of $752M TTM implies $188M/quarter, but this is distorted by the timing of the $1.3B capital raise).

What matters most is the gap between the cash pile ($890M) and the burn trajectory: if the company sustains $160M/qtr in cash consumption, the $890M lasts approximately 5-6 quarters (1.25-1.5 years). If burn accelerates due to certification work, it could be 3-4 quarters.

## 4. Products, Segments, And Moat

### Product Breakdown

NuScale has a single product platform: the VOYGR-12 reactor modules. The VOYGR design uses light-water reactor technology — fundamentally the same nuclear physics as any existing U.S. nuclear plant — but in a significantly smaller footprint. Each module produces 77 MW(e) of electrical output and is housed in a single spent fuel pool in containment structure. The design is passive safe: even without operator intervention or external power, the reactor cools itself through natural convection, similar to the AP1000 by Westinghouse.

The design can be scaled by combining modules:

- Single module: ~77 MW(e) — suitable for industrial sites, mining, military bases, island communities
- 6-module block: ~462 MW(e) — suitable for utility grid replacement
- 12-module block: ~924 MW(e) — suitable for full utility replacement or export (hydrogen production, data center clusters)

No other module configuration is commercially available in the U.S. today.

### Competitive Position

The SMR competitive landscape is fragmented and unproven:

| Competitor | Product | Status | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|---|
| NuScale (SMR) | VOYGR-12 | NRC certification review | Only SMR in active NRC standard design review; first-mover advantage |
| GE-Hitachi | BWRX-300 | Pre-certification; lost multiple bids | Larger module (300 MW); has lost competition to SMR bids |
| TerraPower | Natrium | DOE funding; pre-certification | Bill Gates-backed; molten sodium; different technology |
| X-energy | X-100 | Early stage; DOE support | High-temperature gas reactor; significantly further behind |
| Kirkhammer | Small modular | Early stage | DOE SMR funding; no NRC submission yet |

NuScale has the clearest regulatory path. Its NRC certification review is the only active standard design certification for an SMR in the United States. This is significant because NRC certification is the gatekeeper: without it, no SMR can be commercially deployed at scale in the United States. The certification establishes a precedent that other developers can follow.

On the downside, NuScale has lost its only proven customer (Utah Gen & Power). GE-Hitachi's BWRX-300 hasn't won a competitive bid. TerraPower is dependent on Bill Gates' personal credibility and DOE funding. The competitive advantage is a race with no finish line.

### Moat Assessment

The moat is strongest in the regulatory arena. NRC certification is not just approval — it is a precedent-setting process that creates a template for subsequent applicants. First-mover certification could yield a 2-3 year advantage over competitors, which in the nuclear industry (where certification alone takes 5-8 years) represents the most significant competitive lead possible.

The moat is weakest in commercial execution. NuScale has zero deployed modules. The Boise cancellation demonstrated that customer commitments are fragile: a $2.6 billion LOI is not a purchase order. If certification arrives without a firm order, the moat collapses to a regulatory formality with no economic moat to speak of.

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

Reported earnings are overstated on the expense side and understated on the revenue side, but in the sense that they do not approximate owner earnings. The TTM reported loss of $385.8 million includes substantial non-cash items that distort the picture:

The biggest non-cash adjustment is likely stock-based compensation, which is material in a company that diluted 160% in FY2025. Operating expenses of $716M TTM include this SBC drag, which does not represent actual cash consumption. The actual cash burn (operating cash flow) is even worse: $751.5M TTM, which is $66M more than the net loss.

The key uncertainty is whether operating expenses of $716M are structural or transitional. If these expenses include the tail end of Boise project costs, impairment charges, or one-time transition expenses, they will decline. If they reflect the true cost of running an NRC certification program with 500+ employees, they will persist.

For valuation, I would use cash consumption (operating cash flow) rather than net income. Net income is distorted by non-cash charges and non-GAAP adjustments. The operating cash flow of $751.5M TTM is the real measure of how much capital is being consumed.

Reported earnings are noisy because of: (1) massive dilution-related SBC, (2) non-cash impairment charges from the cancelled Boise project, (3) non-recurring termination revenue in FY2024 that inflated the revenue baseline, (4) one-time gains/losses that distort the income statement. The key issue is that the $112M impairment in FY2023 is a non-cash charge that inflated the net loss without consuming cash, but it represents real economic damage — the sunk investment in the Boise project is gone.

For valuation purposes, I would use the cash NAV floor plus the optionality framework rather than any earnings-based metric. Earnings are meaningless for a company losing $386M per year with no revenue scale.

### Forecast Logic

The bear case assumes NRC certification is delayed beyond 2028, another major project cancellation occurs, and NuScale needs to raise another $500M-$1B before it can begin commercial deployments. Revenue stays near $10-20M per year (consulting, grants, non-core work). The company survives but does nothing other than burn cash. By year 5, the $890M pile is gone, another dilutive raise is required, and the per-share value is approximately $0-2.

The base case assumes NRC certification proceeds on schedule (2027-2028 timeline), but no anchor customer materializes at the time of certification. Revenue grows slowly to $50-100M range through DOE grants, consulting, and licensing activity. The company uses the remaining cash through 2028, then may need another smaller raise. ## 9. Market Debate And Variant Perception

### What Bulls Believe

Bulls see NuScale as the definitive U.S. SMR play. The argument is: NRC certification is inevitable and will happen within the next 2-3 years. Once certified, NuScale will have the first-mover advantage to bid on the growing pipeline of utility SMR projects. The DOE's $199.5M cost-sharing commitment and IRA tax credits provide a policy backstop that de-risks the economics. Utah Gen & Power's cancellation of the Boise project was a market failure, not a technology failure. Natural gas prices and battery economics have improved since 2023, but SMRs are still cheaper than building a new gas turbine for baseload power with no emissions.

The bull case also argues that the cash position ($890M) provides more than sufficient runway. At current burn rates, the company can survive well past certification without another dilutive raise. The $2.89B market cap implies a $6.03 per-share optionality premium over the cash floor — which is reasonable given the potential value of a first-mover SMR certification. The bull case is supported by the growing demand for firm clean power: data centers, industrial electrification, and grid reliability requirements are all driving nuclear interest.

### What Bears Believe

Bears see a company that has already proven it cannot execute a commercially viable nuclear project. The Boise cancellation destroyed $2.6B in potential revenue. The company has lost $386M in the last year with only $18.7M in TTM revenue. The Q1 FY2026 revenue of $0.565M suggests the organic revenue baseline is effectively zero. Every raise has been dilutive. The company has diluted by 580% since the SPAC. At the current burn rate, the $890M will be gone within 2-3 years, requiring another dilutive raise that could cut per-share value by 40-60%.

The bear argument is strongest on the execution record. NuScale's single confirmed purchase order (Boise) was cancelled. The company has no deployed modules. It has no recurring revenue. The "first-mover NRC certification" advantage can only become a commercial advantage if there are actual customers — and the Boise cancellation proved that customer commitments are fragile. The $890M in cash might not be a floor if it's consumed on more certification work.

### What The Market May Be Missing

The market appears to be focused on whether NRC certification is coming. The more important issue may be the gap between certification and commercialization. The BOE/SMR space is one where certification is necessary but not sufficient. Even with certification, NuScale faces: (1) utility procurement cycles that take 0.6+ years, (2) competitive bidding from GE-Hitachi's BWRX-300 and other SMRs, (3) customer concentration risk (a single contract must be won to validate $500M+ in sales per module), and (4) technology competition from battery + gas hybrid solutions that are cheaper and faster to deploy.

The market may be underweighting the timing mismatch: NRC certification will arrive in 2027-2028, but by then utilities will need to have already started procurement cycles. If the first procurement cycle for SMRs has already been won or lost by the time NuScale is certified, the certification arrives too late to be commercially meaningful.

## 15. Risks And Red Flags

The single biggest risk is the cash burn trajectory consuming the $890M pile before first commercial deployment. At a burn rate of $160M per quarter, the runway is approximately 5-6 quarters. The $890M was funded by the FY2025 equity raise at an effective ~$41 per share. A forced raise at $8.60 or lower would dilute existing shareholders by 50-100%.

Specific red flags:

1. **Revenue collapse confirmed**: Q1 FY2026 revenue of $565K is a 95.8% YoY decline. The organic revenue floor appears to be near zero.
2. **Dilution history**: 580% share count expansion since SPAC. The per-share value has been systematically destroyed.
3. **Execution risk**: Single confirmed purchase order (Boise) was cancelled. No other confirmed customer.
4. **Competitive displacement**: GE-Hitachi's BWRX-300, TerraPower's Natrium, and X-energy are all competing for the same utility projects. NuScale is not guaranteed to win the first contract even if certified.
5. **NRC timeline uncertainty**: The standard design certification review for a first-of-kind reactor is expected to take 5-8 years. NuScale started the process in 2017-2018, putting the target completion in 2022-2025 — already late. Any further delay increases the dilution risk.
6. **Macro nuclear economics**: Even with certification, SMRs need to compete with gas turbines, batteries, and large nuclear on economics. The Boise cancellation proved the economics are fragile at current cost levels.
7. **Policy risk**: The DOE and NRC support SMRs, but a government administration change could alter funding priorities or regulatory timelines.
8. **Insider ownership low at 24% is the risk — even if the company succeeds, the per-share value may be limited by dilution.
