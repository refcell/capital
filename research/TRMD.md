## 1. Thesis In One Page

### The Setup

TORM looks cheap on every earnings number the freight market is printing this year, and expensive on every earnings number the same fleet produced twelve months ago. At $41.73 the stock trades at 6.8× TTM diluted EPS of $6.14 and 4.5× FY2026 consensus EPS of $9.21, against a 10.6% trailing yield and a 0.76× net-debt-to-TTM-EBITDA balance sheet. The same stock trades at 14.6× FY2025 EPS of $2.85 and at 1.14× June broker NAV of $36.50. The gap is not a data error. Q2 FY2026 time-charter-equivalent earnings were $512 million on $59,301 per day, versus $208 million on $26,672 per day a year earlier. That is a doubling of the rate, not a doubling of the franchise. The market is not confused about which number is real. It is arguing about how long $50,000-plus days last.

The surface narrative is that product-tanker tightness — Red Sea rerouting, Russian product displacement, and a still-constrained orderbook — has produced a record year, that TORM’s integrated commercial and technical platform is capturing more of the spike than peers, that Oaktree is finally selling into strength, and that a 10% yield pays investors to own a $4 billion hard-asset fleet. The real question is whether $9 of 2026 EPS is a new plateau or a cycle print that mean-reverts toward the $2.85 the company earned in 2025. Street FY2027 EPS of $6.15 already assumes a one-third fade. The October 12 guide raise to $1.7–$1.9 billion of TCE and $1.3–$1.5 billion of EBITDA tells you 2026 will be the peak year in the current numbers. It does not tell you 2027.

The thesis works if mid-cycle TCE/day settles in the mid-to-high $30,000s rather than the mid-$20,000s, if the 12 MR resales and newbuilds arriving 2027–2029 earn their cost of capital instead of delivering into a rate collapse, and if Hafnia’s 20% stake is a commercial endgame rather than a bid that strand the float. It fails if Q3 coverage at $38,606/day is the first step down a slope back to 2025 rates, if second-hand values give back the $1.2 billion of fleet appreciation booked between June 2025 and June 2026, or if the variable dividend is treated by the market as a 10% coupon rather than as a cycle residual.

### The Core Debate

The debate is not whether TORM is a well-run product-tanker company. Q2 showed 44% ROIC, a 73% payout of record net profit, and a second guidance raise in eight weeks. The debate is whether 4.5× 2026 earnings on a doubled stock is a value or a value trap. A 8.5× multiple on $4.50 of mid-cycle EPS is $38.25, 8% below the current price. A 7× multiple on $2.80 of trough EPS is $19.60, 53% below. The stock is already capitalizing a successful defense of a high-cycle earnings base. Upside from here requires either rates that stay near Q2 or a NAV re-rating the June broker book has not yet printed.

## 2. Company Story

### Origin And Evolution

TORM was founded in 1889 in Copenhagen as a steamship company. For most of the twentieth century it was a diversified Danish tramp and liner owner. The modern TORM is a post-restructuring product-tanker specialist. The 2012–2015 shipping crash left the old TORM A/S over-levered. Oaktree-backed OCM Njord recapitalized the franchise, moved the listing vehicle to TORM plc in the United Kingdom, and rebuilt the fleet around MR, LR1 and LR2 product tankers with in-house commercial and technical management. The New York listing (TRMD) sits alongside Nasdaq Copenhagen (TRMD A) on the same ISIN, GB00BZ3CNK81. Headquarters are at 120 Cannon Street in London, with the operating center in Hellerup, Denmark. Jacob Meldgaard has been chief executive through the rebuild.

The economic history that matters for this stock is the 2021–2026 freight cycle, not the 1889 founding. FY2021 revenue was $620 million and the company lost $42 million. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, the redirection of clean-product flows, and later Red Sea disruption turned product tankers into a scarcity asset. Revenue jumped to $1.44 billion in FY2022 and $1.52 billion in FY2023, with net income of $563 million and $648 million. FY2024 held $1.56 billion of revenue and $613 million of net income. FY2025 was the down year inside the up cycle: revenue $1.34 billion, net income $285 million, diluted EPS $2.85. TTM through June 30, 2026 has already reversed that fade — $1.76 billion of revenue, $625 million of net income, $6.14 of diluted EPS — because Q2 2026 was the strongest quarter in the company’s history.

Oaktree’s exit is the corporate-finance overlay. Through 2026 OCM Njord has been selling stock into the rate spike: 9.0 million Class A shares priced at $32.25 in mid-September, then 6.33 million priced at $40.05 on October 6 for about $254 million of gross proceeds to the selling shareholder, closing October 7. Hafnia Limited, itself one of the largest product-tanker owners, has been the other side of that tape, buying 4.5 million shares at $32.25 and a further 1.7 million at $34.00 to reach about 19.85% of TORM. The sponsor is leaving. A strategic competitor is arriving. That is not a quiet ownership transition.

### Why The Company Matters Now

TORM matters because it is one of a handful of pure-play product-tanker equities large enough for U.S. institutions, because its fleet of roughly 100 ships is a real claim on clean-petroleum ton-miles, and because the next two prints — Q3 today and FY2026 in February — will tell the market whether $59,000 days were a quarter or a regime. At 102 million shares and a $4.3 billion equity value it is small enough that a 1-turn change in the mid-cycle multiple, or a $10,000/day change in TCE, moves the stock more than the dividend. It also matters because Hafnia’s 20% stake puts a combination, or at least a commercial overlay, on the table at a moment when Oaktree is no longer the controlling economic owner.

## 3. Business Model

### How The Company Makes Money

TORM owns product tankers and hires them out, mostly on the spot market and on short period charters, to move refined oil products — gasoline, jet fuel, diesel, naphtha, gasoil — and some dirty products and chemicals. Revenue on the income statement is voyage revenue. The number management and the market actually underwrite is time-charter equivalent earnings: voyage revenue minus voyage expenses (bunkers, port costs, commissions). TCE/day times available earning days is the top line of the economic engine. In Q2 2026, 8,519 earning days at $59,301 produced $512 million of TCE on $663 million of IFRS revenue.

The fleet is three classes. MRs (~45–50k dwt) are the workhorses of regional clean-product trades and were 65 of 97 vessels at June 30. LR1s (~75k dwt) sit in the middle, 10 vessels. LR2s (~110–120k dwt) do longer-haul naphtha and clean-product runs, 22 vessels, and earned $66,993/day in Q2 against $57,040 for MRs. A small Marine Engineering segment — $37 million of FY2025 revenue against $1.31 billion in Tanker — develops green marine equipment. It is not the thesis.

Pricing is spot-heavy. As of August 18, 70% of 2026 earning days were covered at $45,391/day, with 10,271 days still open. A $1,000/day move on the open book is about $10 million of EBITDA. That is a feature in a tight market and a bug in a loose one. There is no software-like recurrence. Utilization is high in a tight market because ships that are not in drydock work; the constraint is the rate, not the volume.

### The Economic Engine

The engine is: a tight product-tanker market lifts TCE/day; TORM’s in-house commercial desk puts ships on the highest-paying routes; voyage expenses are mostly bunkers and ports, so incremental TCE falls through to EBITDA at a high rate; depreciation is large and cash interest is modest at 0.8× net-debt-to-EBITDA; leftover cash after maintenance docking and growth capex is paid out quarterly as excess liquidity. The weak link is the rate. Nothing else in the model — not SG&A of $111 million TTM, not interest of $71 million, not a 0.7% tax rate — can save the P&L if TCE/day goes back to $25,000. Q2’s 44% ROIC is the same fleet that earned 10% ROIC in Q2 2025. The ships did not change. The day rate did.

### Unit Economics

The unit is a ship-day. Q2 2026 TCE/day of $59,301 on 8,519 days is the current print. H1 2026 averaged $47,259, which already mixes Q1’s slower start with Q2’s spike. FY2025 implied a much lower average: $910 million of TCE for the year. Cash operating costs plus G&A per day are the breakeven; TORM does not publish a clean cash-breakeven in the Q2 highlights, but FY2025 EBIT of $337 million on $1.34 billion of revenue (25% operating margin) against Q2 2026 EBIT margin of 54% shows the operating leverage. Once bunkers and opex are covered, another $10,000/day on 90 working ships is roughly $300 million of annual EBITDA. That is why the $1,000/day-to-$10 million-EBITDA rule of thumb in the Q2 outlook is the only unit-economic number that matters.

Customer concentration is not disclosed at a useful grain in the public highlights. Oil majors and commodity traders are the demand side. Switching costs are low. A ship that is open next week is for hire next week. The moat, such as it is, is not a customer lock; it is scale, reputation with charterers, and the ability to position a 100-ship book.

That is a bet that mid-cycle MR rates in 2028–2030 still clear the newbuild cost of capital. If they do not, TORM will have spent the cycle’s cash on ships that earn trough returns.

### Competitive Position

The competitive set is other product-tanker owners: Hafnia (already a 20% holder), Scorpio Tankers, International Seaways, Frontline’s product exposure, CMB.Tech, and a long tail of Greek and Asian owners. TORM’s stated edge is integrated in-house commercial and technical management — One TORM — versus pool structures that split commercial control. The evidence for an edge is the Q2 outperformance versus TORM’s own history, not a clean share-gain statistic versus Hafnia or STNG. In a $59,000/day market everyone makes money. The edge, if it exists, shows up in the downcycle as higher utilization, lower opex, and fewer off-hire days. That test has not been run on this fleet mix.

Hafnia’s stake changes the competitive map. A 20% holder that also operates one of the largest overlapping fleets can be a partner, a predator, or a passive financial owner. None of those is priced. A combination would be a scale event. A standoff would leave TORM independent with a large, possibly friendly, block. A hostile path is possible but not signaled.

### Moat Assessment

The moat is strongest in operating scale and charterer relationships, weaker in any structural claim on rates, and the market may be overrating the platform because Q2’s 44% ROIC looks like a franchise when it is mostly a rate. Product tankers are commodities with a quality overlay. TORM cannot keep $59,000 days if the Atlantic Basin has surplus MRs. What it can keep is a younger-than-average MR book, a scrubber and coating configuration that charterers will take first, and a commercial desk that puts ships in the right basin. That is a real but thin moat. It supports a mid-cycle multiple of 8–9× earnings and a small premium to NAV. It does not support 4.5× peak earnings as a permanent cheapness.

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

Earnings quality is clean at the operating line and noisy as a valuation denominator because the cycle is the accounting. Operating income, TCE, and operating cash flow all tell the same story: the ships earned a lot of cash in the last twelve months. Net income of $625 million sits close to operating income of $688 million after $71 million of interest, $8 million of asset-sale gains, and $4 million of tax. There is no CLBT-style non-operating whiplash and no Pfizer-style impairment stack. Stock-based compensation of $34 million is 1.9% of revenue. Gains on sale of assets are $8 million TTM, down from $51 million in FY2024, so the current EPS print is not being carried by ship sales.

The distortions that matter are different. First, TTM and FY2026 EPS are cycle-peak. Using $6.14 or $9.21 as a “run-rate” is the error. FY2025 EPS of $2.85 is the most recent down year and is the better starting point for a mid-cycle conversation, even though 2025 was not a trough in the 2021 sense. Second, reported FCF is an investment-cycle number. Capex of $510 million TTM against D&A of $226 million means TORM is adding ships, not just maintaining them. FCF margin of 11.4% versus an EBITDA margin of 51.9% is the capex, not deteriorating conversion of TCE to cash. OCF-to-net-income of 1.13× is the quality metric; FCF-to-net-income of 0.32× is the growth-capex metric. Third, the 0.70% TTM tax rate is a tonnage-tax regime, not a temporary benefit. FY2025 was 3.12%. Neither is a GAAP industrial tax rate, and neither should be normalized to 21%. Fourth, working capital drained $171 million TTM, including $139 million in inventory. Some of that is bunker timing. A reversal would add to FCF without adding to franchise value.

Rate the earnings as clean / cyclical. The operating engine is real. The P&L is not being manufactured below the operating line. The mistake is treating a $59,000/day quarter as normalized earnings power.

## 7. Forward Projections And Scenario Model

Analyst coverage is thin — three names on FY2026 financials, two on the 12-month price target — so consensus is an anchor, not a crowd. Treat the $1.63 billion as stale relative to the raise. The forecast logic is mean reversion with a high floor, not growth. ## 8. Management, Governance, And Capital Allocation

Jacob Meldgaard is CEO and executive director; Kim Balle is CFO; Mikael Bo Larsen is head of IR. The board sits on a U.K. plc with a Danish operating center and dual Nasdaq listings. Insider ownership is 0.11%. Institutions own about 29%. The float is 82 million shares. Governance in 2026 is about the two large outside holders, not about the founders.

Capital allocation is simple and disclosed. The distribution policy is to pay out excess liquidity quarterly. Q2’s $2.40 dividend was 73% of net profit and $246 million of cash. FY2025 paid $2.12. The trailing $4.42 is the last four distributions, not a board-set coupon. Growth capex is the other use of cash: two 2015 MRs delivered in Q2, six MR resales for 2027–28, six MR newbuilds for 2029 with two 2030 options. Net debt fell from $845 million at FY2025 to $699 million at June 30 even as the company bought ships, because operating cash flow of $710 million TTM was large enough to fund both the orderbook installment and a dividend. ROIC of 44% in Q2 is the allocation scoreboard for a peak quarter; FY2025 ROE of 13.4% is the scoreboard for a down year. Buybacks are not the story. Share count has risen 2.4% year over year on RSUs (132k, 97k and 61k Class A shares in September–October) and on residual equity issuance, not on a repurchase program.

The Oaktree–Hafnia transition is the governance event. OCM Njord selling 15.3 million shares in six weeks is a sponsor who has decided the public market is the exit. Hafnia at 19.85% is a related-industry holder large enough to force a conversation. No combination has been announced. Related-party risk is the usual shipping kind — ship sales, time charters, newbuild slots — and is not visible as a red flag in the Q2 highlights. Employee count of 605 against $1.76 billion of TTM revenue is $2.9 million of revenue per employee, which is what a ship-owning company with seafarers and a small shore staff looks like, not a bloated overhead.

## 9. Market Debate And Variant Perception

Bulls see a scarce product-tanker fleet, a second consecutive guidance raise, a 10% yield, 0.8× leverage, and a stock that still trades at 4.5× 2026 earnings after doubling. They point to Q2 TCE/day of $59,301, to 70% of 2026 days already booked at $45,391, and to Evercore at $45. They argue that even a fade to $6 of 2027 EPS at 8× is $48, so $42 is not a demanding price. They treat Hafnia as a bid, not an overhang.

Bears see peak-on-peak. FY2025 EPS of $2.85 is eleven months old. Q2 coverage for Q3 was already down to $38,606/day. LR1 coverage was $32,608. The stock is 1.14× a June NAV that has not been refreshed. Kepler’s $21 target from May is stale on the numbers and still useful as a reminder that European shipping desks fade this cycle hard. Oaktree selling 6.3 million shares at $40.05 is informed supply. Growth capex into 2029 is how shipping companies over-earn and then over-build.

What the market is missing is not a secret rate forecast. It is the distinction between 2026 EPS and mid-cycle EPS, and the fact that NAV, not FCF, is the floor. A holder who thinks $9.21 is the denominator will find the stock cheap forever until it is not. A holder who thinks $36.50 of June NAV is the floor will not overpay by much at $42, and will not assume a $20 outcome without a genuine rate collapse. The variant is to treat $4.50 of mid-cycle EPS at 8.5× — $38 — as the base, to treat the 10% yield as a 2026 residual that funds the wait, and to wait for Q3 NAV before deciding whether the 14% premium is earned.

## 10. Bull Case

The bull case is that product-tanker ton-miles stay structurally higher than 2019–2021 because Russian barrels, Red Sea risk, and refinery location keep clean products traveling farther, while the MR orderbook remains orderly enough that TORM’s own deliveries do not glut the market. In that world Q2’s $59,000 days are not a run-rate, but $40,000–$45,000 days are. On a 100-plus ship fleet that still produces something like $5.80 of EPS in 2031. A 9× multiple — a scarce-fleet premium, not a growth multiple — is $52. NAV rises with second-hand prices toward $45, and the stock holds a 15% premium to that NAV. Hafnia either bids or commercial cooperation captures more cargo. The dividend stays fat through 2027 because even a faded year looks like 2023–2024, not like 2025. Required: Q3 TCE/day holds near the $38.6k book and the $1.7–$1.9 billion TCE guide is reaffirmed; Q3 NAV prints closer to $40 than to $36.50; 2027 guide, when it comes, is not a cliff.

## 11. Bear Case

The bear case is a rate collapse toward FY2025, not a going-concern failure. TCE/day falls back into the mid-$20,000s as Red Sea transit normalizes, as new MRs (including TORM’s) deliver, and as product inventories stop being reshuffled. FY2031 EPS of $2.80 is a 2025-like year on a slightly larger fleet. A 7× trough multiple is $19.60. Second-hand values give back a third of the June 2025–June 2026 gain, NAV goes to $24, and the stock trades at 0.85× NAV, or $20. The dividend is cut because the policy is excess liquidity, not a coupon, and “excess” shrinks. Interest coverage is still fine — $337 million of FY2025 EBIT against $71 million of TTM interest is 4.7× — so this is a price bear, not a credit bear. Required: Q3 uncovered days price well below $38.6k; the October guide is trimmed or Q4 commentary turns; broker values stall while the stock sits at $42.

## 12. Base Case

The most likely outcome is that 2026 is a record, 2027 is a fade, and 2028–2031 settle in a mid-cycle band that is better than 2025 and worse than 2026. Street FY2027 EPS of $6.15 is still too high for a terminal number if the cycle has further to run down, so the model uses $4.50 of 2031 EPS, roughly halfway from 2025’s $2.85 to 2027’s $6.15. An 8.5× multiple — in line with how product-tanker equities are priced when they are neither distressed nor euphoric — is $38.25, 8% below the current price. NAV holds around $36.50 in real terms as ship values cool with rates but the fleet grows, and the stock earns a small premium to that NAV. The dividend in 2027 is lower than $4.42 and higher than FY2025’s $2.12. Total return from $41.73 is roughly the faded yield minus a small de-rate. That is a cost-of-capital hold, not an entry.

Already-released Q2 is not a remaining catalyst. The October 12 guide raise is in the price. Evercore’s $45 target is in the price for anyone still using 2026 EPS as the denominator.

## 15. Risks And Red Flags

The biggest risk is that $59,000 TCE days were a dislocation and that the equity, having doubled, now discounts a permanence the freight market will not deliver. That is a price risk of 30–50%, not a solvency risk. Leverage is low, interest coverage is high, and the ships have a bid in the second-hand market.

Other risks are real but secondary. Growth capex into 2029 can strand capital if MR rates fade. The 10.6% yield will be cut if the policy is followed, and holders who bought the yield will sell the stock. Hafnia at 20% can freeze the register or force a deal at a NAV price rather than a peak-EPS price. Oaktree’s remaining line, if still material, is supply. Older LR2s face CII and FuelEU capex. Tonnage tax is a political residual. Working-capital swings can make a single quarter’s FCF look better or worse than the franchise. Thin coverage means the $45 target can move $10 on one note. Share count creeps up through RSUs. Marine Engineering is a non-issue unless it starts absorbing cash.

A red flag that is not present: there is no preferred stock, no goodwill bomb, no customer that is 40% of revenue in the disclosed numbers, and no Key-Stats-versus-balance-sheet debt gap. The books match.

## 18. Conclusion

TORM is a well-run product-tanker company having a record year, listed at a 14% premium to a June NAV of $36.50 and at 4.5× a 2026 EPS number that the company itself is guiding to a peak. The probability-weighted fair value is $35.45. The stance is Watch. The main reason to care is the combination of a hard-asset floor, a variable 10% trailing yield, and a possible Hafnia endgame. The main reason to be careful is that the stock has already doubled, Q3 coverage is booking $20,000/day below Q2, and 4.5× peak earnings is not the same thing as cheap. The next trigger is this morning’s Q3 report: TCE versus the $38,606 book, the October guide, and the broker NAV. Until those three numbers are public, adding to a cycle-high print is a bet on 2026 lasting, not a bet on TORM.
