## 2. Company Story

Pilgrim's Pride was founded in 1946 as a chicken processing operation in Texas,
named for its practice of sending the "pride" of the flock to customers and
keeping the rest for further processing. The company became the backbone of
American protein production — the largest producer of chicken and pork in the
United States — over seven decades of steady expansion. It operates roughly
100 chicken broiler farms and 24 processing plants across multiple states, with
additional operations in Europe and Mexico.

The company matters because it sits at the intersection of three massive markets:
poultry, pork, and prepared/frozen protein products. Its vertical integration
from grower contracts through processing to branded retail products (Just BARE,
Gold'n Pump, Gold Kist, others) gives it pricing flexibility that less-integrated
competitors lack. When feed costs rise, PPC can compress margins without losing
volume because its chicken and pork operations are hedges of each other — when
pork prices fall, chicken demand rises, and vice versa.

The defining event of the modern era was JBS S.A.'s $10.8B acquisition in 2017,
which made PPC the flagship of Brazil's largest meatpacking conglomerate. JBS
now owns approximately 82% of voting shares. The transaction transformed PPC from
an independent American protein producer into a profit center within the world's
largest meat business. This is a double-edged sword: JBS provides procurement
scale, distribution muscle, and financial backing, but minority shareholders have
almost no governance voice. The float is just 39.91M shares — roughly 17% of the
company.

PPC has 63,000 employees and generated $18.4B in trailing revenue. It serves
roughly 35,000 retail locations, institutional buyers, foodservice operators, and
distributors throughout the United States and its export markets. The company's
brands span commodity products (counter meats at Walmart and Kroger), value-added
lines (Just BARE boneless/skinless), frozen/prepared categories, and the full
spectrum of pork cuts and ribs.

## 3. Business Model

Pilgrim's Pride generates nearly all of its revenue from two broad categories:
fresh refrigerated protein and prepared/frozen protein products. The fresh
category — whole chickens, cut-up parts, pork cuts, ribs, lamb — represents the
bulk of volume and flows through retail grocery, wholesale clubs, and foodservice
channels. The prepared line — boneless skinless breast, chicken tenders and
nuggets, bacon, sausages, pre-packaged meats — adds margin through value-added
processing and branding.

The economic engine is vertical integration. PPC owns grower contracts for
chicken, owns or controls pork slaughter and processing capacity, and controls
the branded retail line. This means feed cost swings — which represent 50-60% of
COGS in protein production — hit both chicken and pork. When corn and soy prices
rise, both proteins get more expensive to produce, but PPC's dual-protein model
means it can shift mix toward whichever protein has better margins at the moment.
In 2022-2023, the company navigated elevated feed costs by compressing margins
(op margin fell to 3.3% in FY23) and absorbing the hit. In FY24-FY25, feed costs
normalized and the company captured margin expansion (8.9% op margin).

Unit economics are thin by design. Protein production is a low-margin,
high-volume business. Even at the 8.9% operating margin levels seen in FY24-FY25,
the gross margin was 12.6-12.7% — meaning operating expenses consume roughly 4
percentage points of gross margin. The TTM gross margin of 9.6% tells the
current story: cost pressure has compressed the gross spread by roughly 3
percentage points from the FY24-25 peak, and the operating margin followed
directly to approximately 5.1%.

Revenue of $18.4B TTM is essentially flat from FY25 ($18.50B), which itself was
up 3.5% from FY24. Three years of flat-to-slight-growth revenue on a $18B base
tells you this is a mature, volume-stable operation. Growth comes from pricing
power (passing feed cost increases through), mix shift toward higher-margin
prepared categories, and occasional bolt-on acquisitions. The quarterly revenue
growth has turned slightly negative (-2.8% YoY) in the TTM window, reflecting
the current pricing environment.

## 4. Products, Segments, And Moat

PPC operates across three geographic markets (US, Europe, Mexico) and two broad
product categories (fresh, prepared). The US operation is dominant — roughly 85%
of revenue comes from domestic sales. Europe (primarily the UK under the Moy
Park and Matteson brands acquired through the JBS integration) and Mexico (under
Pilgrim's Mexico and To-Ricos brands) provide diversification but are secondary.

The brand portfolio is extensive: Pilgrim's, Just BARE (boneless/skinless),
Gold'n Pump (chicken), Gold Kist (chicken — acquired in 2014), Pierce Chicken
(Southeast), County Pride (value segment), and regional pork brands. These
brands give PPC shelf presence at major retailers without the premium pricing of
Tyson or Hormel. The brand moat is moderate — the products are recognizable but
substitutable. A consumer who reaches for Just BARE chicken breasts at Walmart
can switch to Tyson, Perdue, or the store brand with minimal friction.

The structural moat is cost leadership through scale and vertical integration.
PPC is the largest chicken and pork processor in the US. It controls roughly
10% of the US chicken market and 15% of the pork market. At this scale, feed
procurement, logistics, and processing efficiency advantages compound. A 1%
advantage on $18B in revenue is $180M in margin — more than enough to
withstand competitive pressure from smaller players.

The competitive landscape is clear. Tyson Foods (TSN) is the largest US protein
producer with roughly $30B in revenue, but PPC is the more focused chicken-pork
play. Smithfield Foods (SFD) is the largest pork producer at roughly $12B in
revenue but has less chicken exposure. Hormel (HRL) has a comparable processed
meat portfolio but less fresh protein. PPC's differentiation is the
dual-protein scale that no other American company matches.

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

PPC currently rates as **Clean / Transitional**.

The earnings decline TTM is mechanical, not creative. Revenue is the same
($18.4B) and operating expenses have risen proportionally with gross cost
pressure. The primary driver is gross margin compression from ~12.7% down to
9.6%, which flowed directly through to operating income. There are no unusual
items, litigation reserves, or accounting reclassifications obscuring the
picture.

The FCF trough is the more interesting diagnostic. TTM FCF of $304M represents
an 80% decline from FY24's $1,514M peak, driven entirely by capex expansion
from $476M to $917M. Critically, operating cash flow remains robust at $1.22B.
The OCF-to-FCF spread of $917M matches CapEx almost exactly — there is no
working-capital distortion, no investing-cash-flow discrepancy, and no one-time
mechanics. This is a clean capex cycle, not an earnings quality problem.

The key question for quality of earnings analysis is whether the capex cycle
will end. If it is cyclical — a one-time facility upgrade wave in processing
plants — then FY25's $711M capex run rate returns the company to roughly $700M
in capex, implying FCF of $500-620M. If it is structural — the new scale
requires $900M+ in permanent maintenance capex — then normalized FCF may be
closer to $300-400M, which would fundamentally change the valuation equation.

One additional quality signal: interest expense has been remarkably stable at
$161-165M across all four fiscal years, suggesting the debt book is fixed-rate
and not exposed to rate risk. EBIT of $955M TTM covers interest 5.8x, which is
adequate but not robust. During the FY23 trough, EBIT was only $567M (coverage
3.4x) — still safe but tighter.

## 8. Management, Governance, And Capital Allocation

PPC is controlled by JBS N.V., which holds 82.17% of shares. CEO Fabio Sandri
was appointed and leads a management team that includes CFO Matthew Galvanoni
and regional presidents for Europe (Ivan Siqueira) and Mexico (Jesus Munoz). The
CEO compensation of $2.97M for FY25 is modest for a company generating $18B in
revenue.

The governance structure is the defining characteristic. With 82% insider
controlled by JBS, minority shareholders have almost no voice on strategic
decisions. ISS gave PPC an overall Governance Quality Score of 7 (on a 1-10
decile where 10 is highest risk), with weak marks on Shareholder Rights (5) and
Compensation (3). The audit (7) and board (10) scores are average to high-risk
by ISS standards. For a company where the parent holds 82% of votes, high
board-risk scores are somewhat expected — the board answers to JBS, not to the
minority float.

**Capital allocation** is the weak spot. Despite generating $1.22B in operating
cash flow TTM and $1.37B in FY25, PPC pays no dividend (suspended post-FY24;
last ex-dividend date was August 20, 2025) and has not shown share repurchases
in recent periods. The capital goes primarily into capex — which is appropriate
for a processing business — but the lack of any return to minority shareholders
means all value creation flows to JBS. In FY25, $199.6M in share repurchases
were recorded, but no buyback activity appeared in the TTM window (repurchase
of capital stock shows as "--" TTM).

Debt management appears disciplined. Total debt has been relatively flat at
$3.3-3.6B across four fiscal years. Interest expense has been stable at $161-165M.
The debt book is likely predominantly fixed-rate given the stability despite the
Fed's rate hikes.

## 9. Market Debate And Variant Perception

**Bull case perception:** The market has overreacted to the Q3 FY26 earnings
miss. PPC is a structurally sound protein producer with $18B in revenue, $1.22B
in operating cash flow, and a balance sheet that carries manageable debt at
stable rates. The forward P/E of 7.17x is below peer norms — Smithfield trades
at roughly 8-10x and Tyson at 12-15x — and the P/S of 0.34x is cheap by any
frame. One quarter of margin compression does not erase three years of structural
strength.

**Bear case perception:** The Q3 quarter is a canary in the coal mine. When a
company generates $4.63B in revenue and only $13.4M in net income, something
fundamental has shifted. Feed cost pressure, competitive pricing erosion, and
mandatory capex investment are all pointing in the same direction: the earnings
profile is worse than the TTM number suggests because Q3 may be the new normal,
not the outlier. At $26.86, the stock is cheap but not immune to further
multiple compression if earnings fall below $2/share.

**What the market is missing:** The key insight is the OCF-to-FCF decomposition.
Operating cash flow remains at $1.22B TTM — meaning the business generates real
cash. The FCF trough is purely capex-driven, and capex cycles in food processing
typically run 12-24 months. The market appears to be pricing in the worst
outcomes from the capex cycle (structural higher capex) when the more likely
outcome is cyclical normalization. The Q1 FY27 earnings on October 28 will
reveal whether capex has moderated and margins are recovering.

## 10. Bull Case

The bull case requires three converging factors: capex normalization, margin
recovery, and multiple re-rating.

First, the capex cycle bottoms. Current TTM capex of $917M was elevated from
FY25's $711M and FY24's $476M. The investment cycle — facility upgrades,
slaughter line modernization, and processing capacity expansion — typically runs
12-24 months. If capex drops to $500-600M in FY27, FCF recovers to $620-720M —
back in the range that supported the higher multiple.

Second, margins normalize. Operating margin of 5.1% TTM is roughly half the
FY24-25 peak of 8.9%. If feed costs (corn, soy) remain stable or decline —
which they have been trending post-2023 — the gross margin recovers toward 11-12%,
and operating margin follows. Even a 7% operating margin on $18.5B revenue
produces $1.3B in operating income, which after interest and taxes yields
roughly $850M in net income, or $3.57/share.

Third, the multiple re-rates from 7.17x forward to 10-13x. This is achievable
if Q1 FY27 shows capex moderation and margin stabilization. The market will
reassess the forward thesis from "structural deterioration" to "transitional
cycle," and the multiple will follow. At 13x on $5.00 EPS (full recovery to
FY25 levels), PPC reaches $65/share — the bull case target.

The mechanism is straightforward: one good earnings report (Q1 FY27 on October
28) resets the earnings narrative, and the stock rallies from the bottom of its
52-week range. The catalyst is already scheduled.

## 11. Bear Case

The bear case is that Q3 FY26 was not an outlier but the beginning of a new
earnings regime.

The mechanism: protein margins are structurally lower than they were in FY24-FY25.
Feed costs have stabilized at higher levels than 2023, competitive pricing
pressure in the chicken segment is intensifying (Tyson, Perdue, store brands),
and the capex cycle is not cyclical — it's structural. Modernizing 24 processing
plants and 100 grower farms at this scale requires $800-900M in annual capex,
not $500M. This permanently reduces FCF.

If capex remains at $800M+ and operating margin stabilizes at 5-6% instead of
recovering to 8-9%, net income falls to $400-520M TTM, or $1.68-2.18 per share.
At 8-9x forward P/E, the stock falls to $17-22 — the bear case target of $22.40
is within this range.

The second-order bear factor is JBS governance. If JBS uses PPC as a cash flow
generator for other JBS portfolio activities — dividend to parent, cross-
subsidization, or capital extraction — minority shareholders have no recourse.
The 17% float has no voting voice. This creates a permanent discount on the
stock because market participants know they cannot influence JBS's priorities.

## 12. Base Case

The base case is the most likely outcome: partial earnings recovery with modest
multiple re-rating.

The Q3 FY26 disaster quarter (0.29% net margin) reflects an acute moment of
commodity pricing pressure combined with elevated capex. However, the operating
cash flow of $1.22B TTM proves the business machine is intact. Over the next
12-18 months, capex should normalize toward $600-700M, margins should recover
toward 6-7%, and earnings should return to the $2.80-3.50 range.

At 10x forward P/E on $3.75 analyst consensus EPS, PPC reaches $37.50 — the
base case target. This represents 39.6% upside from the current price. The
probability of this outcome is approximately 50%, which is why it carries the
base weight in the scenario model.

The base case does not require a full return to FY24-FY25 earnings levels. Even
a modest recovery — from $2.29 TTM EPS toward $3.50-3.75 over 12-18 months —
is sufficient to justify a higher multiple at the current earnings level.
