## 1. Thesis In One Page

### The Setup

Carlyle is being priced as if its fee stream were a cyclical side business attached to a carry lottery ticket. At $38.59, the market value of $13.75 billion compares with $1.26 billion of trailing fee-related earnings, $2.41 billion of net accrued performance revenue (carry already earned on paper but not yet realized) and $3.35 billion of balance sheet investments attributable to shareholders. Strip out after-tax carry, the investment portfolio and corporate net debt, and the market is paying roughly $27.70 a share for the fee business, or about 10 times after-tax FRE. That is a value-manager multiple for a $485 billion platform that just reported record FRE, record transaction fees, its best DE quarter in nearly four years and $55.8 billion of inflows over twelve months. (Carlyle Q2 2026 8-K Exhibit 99.2 and earnings presentation, August 5, 2026; author calculation.)

The market is not ignoring those numbers. It has re-rated the whole alternative-asset group lower: Morgan Stanley noted alternative managers were down about 27% year to date when it cut its Carlyle target on October 16, and Carlyle itself fell from $50.64 before its August 5 report to $38.59 today, a 24% decline after a beat. The worries are specific. Private credit is facing questions about AI-disrupted software borrowers and loose underwriting; Carlyle's own head of research publicly warned about AI financing risk in October. Private-equity exits have been slow across the industry, which delays carry. And Carlyle in particular has a long history of trading at a discount to Blackstone, KKR and Apollo because its fee growth has lagged and its earnings lean more heavily on lumpy buyout carry. (StockAnalysis news feed: TheFly, CNBC, Reuters items dated September-October 2026; Investing.com Q2 2026 call summary.)

The debate, then, is whether Carlyle's 2026 numbers mark the start of the "fundraising super cycle" management keeps describing, or a peak in transaction fees and realizations that will fade as markets cool. The consensus itself is split: FY2026 consensus EPS of $3.54 implies only about $1.58 of DE in the second half against $1.96 already earned in the first half, while FY2027 consensus of $4.98 implies a 41% jump. Those two numbers cannot both be conservative. The thesis works if Q3 and Q4 show fee growth from flagship fundraising, steady realizations and no credit blow-ups. It fails if management fees keep shrinking and the record Q2 fee quarter proves to be a one-off. (StockAnalysis forecast, October 23, 2026; Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings presentation.)

### Current View

Carlyle screens as one of the cheaper ways to own a large-scale private-markets platform. The probability-weighted five-year fair value in the model is $58.97, 53% above the current price before dividends, with a bear case of $32.40 and a base case of $62.10. A simpler sum-of-the-parts check that gives the fee business only 15 times after-tax FRE lands at $52.76, almost exactly on the $52.21 analyst average. The stance is **Watch / Positive** rather than Buy for one reason: the near-term evidence is not yet clean. ## 2. Company Story

### Origin And Evolution

Carlyle was founded in Washington, D.C. in 1987 by William Conway, Daniel D'Aniello and David Rubenstein. It built its early reputation on buyouts in regulated and government-adjacent industries such as aerospace, defense and government services, and that heritage still shows in the firm's sector focus today. Carlyle's global head of aerospace, defense and government services was on Bloomberg in September 2026 discussing investor demand for defense technology at the firm's Global Investor Conference in Washington. The founders remain significant holders: insiders own 27.4% of the shares, according to StockAnalysis. (StockAnalysis statistics and news feed, September-October 2026.)

The firm went public on May 3, 2012 as a partnership and later converted to a corporation, which broadened its shareholder base and allowed index inclusion. Over the 2010s Carlyle expanded beyond corporate private equity into real estate, infrastructure and natural resources, credit and fund-of-funds and secondaries through AlpInvest. In 2022 the balance sheet shows a $618 million purchase of intangibles and a $150 million cash acquisition, consistent with the purchase of CLO management contracts from CBAM Partners that year. Intangibles jumped from $21.6 million to $793.9 million and have amortized down to $440.9 million since. (StockAnalysis balance sheet and cash flow, FY2021-June 2026.)

The current chapter began in early 2023, when Harvey Schwartz, formerly president of Goldman Sachs, became chief executive after a period of leadership turnover. His program has had four visible parts: grow fee-related earnings and cut their dependence on carry; build a capital markets business that earns transaction fees across the platform; push into private wealth through evergreen products; and return more capital through buybacks. The segment structure now reflects that: Global Private Equity, Global Credit and Carlyle AlpInvest (renamed from Global Investment Solutions). Results are visible. FRE rose from $1,104.6 million in FY2024 to $1,236.2 million in FY2025 and $1,260.0 million on a trailing basis, and the Q2 2026 call highlighted record capital markets fees of more than $100 million, which management attributed directly to repositioning that business three years ago. (Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings presentation and call transcript, August 5, 2026.)

### Why The Company Matters Now

The timing matters because Carlyle's fund cycle and the market cycle are pulling in different directions. Management says nearly all core strategies will be in the market raising capital over the next two years, including the next U.S. buyout fund (which already has a $5 billion anchor), secondaries, portfolio finance, credit opportunities and a defense and re-industrialization platform. Those raises are what drive management fees two to three years out. At the same time, the market has de-rated alternative managers on fears about private credit and slow exits. If the fundraising arrives, Carlyle's fee base will step up just as the shares are priced for stagnation.

## 3. Business Model

Carlyle earns money in three layers. The first is management fees, charged mostly on committed or invested capital in closed-end funds and on net asset value in evergreen and credit vehicles. These fees are contractual, usually for ten years or more in drawdown funds, and form the base of FRE. The second layer is fee-related performance revenue and transaction and portfolio advisory fees: incentive fees on evergreen wealth and credit vehicles that are paid regularly, plus capital markets fees earned when Carlyle arranges financing or underwriting for its portfolio companies. The third layer is carried interest, usually about 20% of fund profits above a hurdle, which is realized only when investments are sold. Carlyle also earns principal investment income on its own balance sheet investments, mostly fund commitments and CLO risk-retention positions.

The economic engine is simple: raise a fund, charge fees for a decade, deploy capital, and harvest carry in the back half of the fund's life. Carlyle's challenge has been that the first part of the engine, fee growth, has been slow. Segment fund management fees were $589.6 million in Q2 2025 and $560.1 million in Q2 2026, a 5% decline even though fee-earning AUM rose 3% to $334.4 billion. The likely reason is that older buyout funds are stepping down from committed to invested capital as they harvest, while new flagship funds have not yet activated. That is why the record $110.5 million of transaction fees and $88.7 million of fee-related performance revenue mattered so much in Q2: they replaced the management-fee shortfall and pushed FRE to $357.7 million. (Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings presentation.)

### Unit Economics

The FRE margin is the clearest unit-economics measure. It was 46% in 2024, 47% in 2025 and 47% in Q2 2026, on segment fee revenue of $759.3 million in the quarter. CFO Justin Plouffe guided the 2026 compensation ratio to roughly 47%, consistent with 2025, because the firm is investing in people, AI and technology and the wealth platform. He said margin should "tick up" in 2027 and 2028 as fundraising flows through. Every point of margin on roughly $2.7 billion of annual segment fee revenue is about $27 million of FRE, or about $0.06 per share after tax. (Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings call, August 5, 2026.)

Carry economics are more volatile. Net accrued performance revenue was $2.41 billion at June 30, 2026, net of $4.5 billion of accrued compensation and $92 million of giveback obligations. Management described it as nearly $7 of pre-tax earnings per share; dividing by 356.33 million shares gives $6.78. That pool fell from $2.86 billion at December 31, 2025, mainly because of carry realizations and lower accrued carry in the seventh U.S. buyout fund. Realized net performance revenue was $357.3 million in FY2025 and $277.3 million over the trailing four quarters. (Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings presentation; author calculation.)

### Competitive Position

Carlyle competes for institutional and wealth capital with Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Ares, Brookfield and TPG, and in secondaries with Ardian, Lexington and others. Its advantages are a long buyout record in specific sectors (aerospace, defense, government services, industrials, healthcare and Japan), a large CLO platform, and AlpInvest, which is a top-tier secondaries and co-investment franchise. Its disadvantages are scale in perpetual capital and insurance, where Apollo and KKR have built much larger balance-sheet-linked platforms, and a slower start in private wealth. Perpetual capital is 34% of Carlyle's fee-earning AUM; peers with insurance affiliates are much higher.

Carlyle also stands out for returning capital. Management said it distributed nearly $7 billion to fund investors in Q2 and $37 billion over the past year, and that its U.S. buyout strategy returned 23% of its fair value to investors over twelve months, more than double the industry rate. In a market where limited partners are short of cash because exits have been slow, a manager that returns capital is better placed to raise the next fund. That is the strongest evidence for the fundraising "super cycle" claim. (Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings call.)

### Moat Assessment

The moat is real but narrower than the largest peers'. Carlyle's brand, its sector teams and its forty-year LP relationships make it a default allocation for many pensions and sovereign funds, and switching costs in drawdown funds are effectively the fund's life. But the moat protects the existing fee stream more than it guarantees growth. Fee growth depends on fund performance relative to peers, and Global Private Equity AUM is down 1% year over year because realizations are outpacing new commitments. AlpInvest's growth (AUM up 16% YoY, FRE up 27%) is the best evidence that Carlyle can win new capital in the categories LPs want. Global Credit's carry funds show a 1.3x gross multiple and 11% gross / 7% net IRR, adequate rather than distinctive. (Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings presentation, Global Credit fund performance table.)

### Latest Period

Q2 2026 was a strong quarter on every segment measure. ### Balance Sheet

Two balance sheets matter, and the data providers show the wrong one for valuation. The consolidated balance sheet carries $28.2 billion of assets and $14.25 billion of debt, but $10.8 billion of that debt is loans payable of consolidated funds, mostly CLO notes secured by CLO collateral and without recourse to Carlyle. Carlyle's operating-entity balance sheet at June 30, 2026 shows $1,256.5 million of cash, $2,648.5 million of debt obligations (excluding about $350 million of loans used to finance CLO investments and $451 million of leases), $3,352.4 million of investments attributable to shareholders and $2,414.6 million of net accrued performance revenue. The $1.0 billion revolver was undrawn. Common equity was $5.24 billion, or $14.83 per share. (Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings presentation, total segment balance sheet and consolidating balance sheet; StockAnalysis balance sheet.)

On that corporate basis Carlyle has $1.39 billion of net debt against $1.26 billion of trailing FRE, which is about 1.1 times FRE. The balance sheet investments ($9.41 per share) and net accrued carry ($6.78 per share pre-tax) together are worth more than four times corporate net debt. This is a lightly levered company.

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

Carlyle's earnings quality is **clean at the fee level, noisy at the GAAP level, and lumpy at the DE level**. Each layer needs a different reading.

GAAP earnings are not useful for valuation. TTM GAAP EPS of $0.97 compares with TTM DE of $3.93 per share. The gap comes from three things. First, unrealized performance allocations swing with fund marks: they were -$606.7 million in Q3 2025, +$967.5 million in Q4 2025, -$681.1 million in Q1 2026 and +$44.7 million in Q2 2026. Second, GAAP includes equity-based compensation, which was $414.1 million over the trailing twelve months. Third, consolidated funds add revenue, expense and non-controlling-interest income that belong to fund investors. The 39.7x trailing GAAP P/E on StockAnalysis is a product of those mechanics, not of a weak business. (Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings presentation; StockAnalysis cash flow.)

GAAP cash flow is even more misleading. StockAnalysis shows -$4.0 billion of TTM operating cash flow and -$4.1 billion of free cash flow, which produces a "-30% FCF yield." Nearly all of that is purchases of investments by consolidated CLOs, which run through operating cash flow and are funded by the CLOs' own borrowings, shown as $4.8 billion of net debt issuance in financing cash flow. Neither the investments nor the debt are Carlyle's in an economic sense. The right cash measure is DE: pre-tax DE of $1.60 billion over the trailing year, from which Carlyle paid $504.5 million of dividends and bought back $915.3 million of stock. (StockAnalysis cash flow; Carlyle Q2 2026 consolidating balance sheet.)

DE itself has two quality issues. The first is equity compensation. DE excludes stock-based compensation, which was about 15% of trailing segment fee revenue. That cost shows up as dilution instead: Carlyle has spent heavily on buybacks, but the share count has fallen only from 357.4 million at December 31, 2025 to 353.4 million at June 30, 2026, and StockAnalysis shows shares up 1.2% year over year on its filing-date count. A fair reading treats part of the buyback as offsetting equity grants, not as a pure return of capital. The second issue is the mix of fee types. Of the $357.7 million Q2 FRE, $110.5 million came from transaction and portfolio advisory fees and $88.7 million from fee-related performance revenue, both records. Transaction fees depend on deal and financing activity, and fee-related performance revenue depends on returns in evergreen and credit vehicles. They are higher-quality than carry, because they are earned and paid regularly, but lower-quality than management fees. The quarter's FRE growth came entirely from those two lines while management fees fell.

Carry is the lumpiest layer. The $2.41 billion of net accrued carry is real value, but realization timing depends on exit markets, and the pool shrank by $444 million in six months. Carlyle's carry is also concentrated: Corporate Private Equity holds $1,017 million of the $2,415 million.

Taken together: FRE of roughly $2.79 per share after a 21% tax rate is the recurring floor; carry realizations and principal income add roughly $1.15 per share on a trailing basis; GAAP should be ignored except as a mark-to-market signal on carry.

### Reverse Valuation

At $38.59, the market is paying about 9.8 times trailing DE. If DE per share stayed flat at $3.93 forever and Carlyle kept paying $1.40 a year, the shares would offer a 10.2% earnings yield, close to the 12.8% WACC the model computes using a 1.87 beta. In other words, the price is consistent with no growth and a high risk premium. Viewed by parts, the market price less $9.41 of investments and $5.35 of after-tax carry, plus $3.91 of net debt, leaves $27.74 per share for the fee business, or about 9.9 times after-tax FRE of $2.79. To justify only today's price, Carlyle needs FRE to stay roughly flat and accrued carry to be realized at a modest haircut. The bear case, which assumes almost no FRE growth for six years, still produces $32.40, 16% below today.

### Takeaway

The scenario model is asymmetric because the starting multiple is low and the balance sheet is clean. Downside comes from multiple compression on flat earnings, not from leverage or insolvency risk. Upside needs only mid-single-digit FRE growth and a return to the 11-12x DE multiple the shares held for much of 2024-2025: StockAnalysis shows forward P/E of 11.80x at the end of FY2024 and 12.90x at the end of FY2025.

## 8. Management, Governance, And Capital Allocation

Harvey Schwartz has run Carlyle since early 2023 and has delivered the operational parts of his plan: FRE has grown, capital markets fees have become a meaningful earnings line, AlpInvest and wealth have grown quickly, and the firm has kept returning capital to fund investors. CFO Justin Plouffe has been clear about the cost side, guiding the 2026 compensation ratio to about 47% and pointing to 2027-2028 for margin expansion. Management references a three-year strategic plan presented at an investor day; its specific targets were not reviewed for this report and are listed as an open question in the model.

Capital allocation has favored buybacks. Over the trailing twelve months Carlyle repurchased $915.3 million of stock and paid $504.5 million of dividends, together about 89% of pre-tax DE. In Q2 2026 alone it repurchased or withheld 6.7 million shares for $304 million, and $1.6 billion remained under the $2.0 billion authorization the board reset in February 2026. The dividend is a fixed $1.40 a year rather than a payout ratio, which equals about 36% of trailing DE. Buying back stock at under ten times DE is accretive, and it is the right use of capital at this price, provided equity grants do not absorb most of it. (Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings presentation; StockAnalysis cash flow.)

Governance risks are modest but present. The founders still hold a large stake, which aligns interests but concentrates influence. Leadership turnover before Schwartz hurt fundraising momentum, so continuity matters. The firm's decision in October 2026 to stop pursuing Lukoil's international assets removed a sanctions-heavy, reputationally complicated deal; the cost, if any, of the pursuit has not been disclosed. Management also told analysts it prefers a capital-light model, while being willing to commit balance sheet capital where returns justify it, such as a seed commitment alongside the next U.S. buyout fund and an infrastructure investment. (Reuters and TheFly via StockAnalysis, October 9, 2026; Carlyle Q2 2026 earnings call.)

## 9. Market Debate And Variant Perception

**Bulls** argue that Carlyle is the cheapest large alternative manager on any earnings measure, that 2026 marks the start of a fundraising super cycle with nearly every flagship in market, that AlpInvest and wealth give it exposure to the fastest-growing categories, and that the firm's record of returning capital will help it win LP allocations. They point to record FRE, record DE in Global Credit and AlpInvest, a 57% increase in pending fee-earning AUM, and a $2.4 billion carry pool.

**Bears** argue that core management fees are shrinking, that the Q2 FRE record relied on transaction fees that will fade, that the consensus for the rest of 2026 is falling, that private credit is entering a credit cycle with AI-disrupted borrowers, and that Carlyle has never closed its valuation gap with peers. BofA's Underperform and Wells Fargo's "entrenched discount" call capture this view.

**What the market may be missing** is how little the current price assumes. The bear argument is mostly about growth, but the price already implies none. The fee business is valued near ten times after-tax FRE after separating carry and investments, which is the multiple of a traditional asset manager in outflow, while Carlyle has had $55.8 billion of inflows in twelve months and growing fee-earning AUM. Second, the market's focus on the 5% management-fee decline misses the timing: fee step-downs in harvesting funds happen before new flagships activate, and management has a $5 billion anchor for the next U.S. buyout fund and $28 billion of pending fee-earning AUM. The variant view is not that Carlyle will re-rate to Blackstone's multiple. It is that a re-rating from about 10x to 11-12x DE, combined with mid-single-digit DE growth and buybacks, delivers a strong return without heroic assumptions.

## 10. Bull Case

The bull case starts with fundraising. Over 2027-2028 the next U.S. buyout fund closes at or above its predecessor's size, AlpInvest's secondaries and portfolio finance flagships raise larger successor funds, credit opportunities and the defense and re-industrialization platform add new pools, and the Unum reinsurance transaction, which analysts on the Q2 call expect to lift insurance-solutions AUM when it closes later in 2026, restarts growth in that business. Management fees turn from a 5% decline to high-single-digit growth by 2027.

That fee growth falls through at a higher margin. Management guided margin expansion for 2027-2028, and in this case FRE margin rises from 47% toward 50% as the investments in technology and wealth distribution mature. Evergreen wealth products, already $20 billion and growing more than 60% a year, keep adding fee-related performance revenue. FRE compounds at 12% a year to about $2.44 billion in FY2031.

Carry adds on top. Exit markets normalize, the Japan and U.S. buyout funds realize steadily, and net accrued carry rebuilds above $3 billion. After-tax DE reaches $7.00 per share in FY2031 as buybacks cut the share count to 320 million. The market rewards the shift toward fee earnings with a 13x DE multiple, in line with Carlyle's forward multiple at the end of 2025. The five-year target is $91.00, 136% above today before dividends.

What has to be true: flagship fundraising succeeds, credit portfolios avoid a meaningful loss cycle, and FRE growth becomes visible in management fees rather than only in transaction fees.

## 11. Bear Case

The bear case starts with fees. Fundraising arrives but is slower and smaller: the next U.S. buyout fund raises less than hoped as LPs consolidate commitments with the largest managers, and fee step-downs in older funds keep management fees flat. Transaction fees fall back from Q2's $110.5 million record as financing activity slows. FRE grows only 1% a year to $1.31 billion in FY2031.

Credit adds pressure. Private credit defaults rise among software and services borrowers exposed to AI disruption, Carlyle's direct-lending and opportunistic credit funds take marks, and fee-related performance revenue in credit vehicles slips. The insurance-solutions business grows slowly beyond the Unum transaction. Carry stays weak: exits remain scarce, accrued carry keeps shrinking, and realized carry runs well below the 2024-2025 pace.

After-tax DE falls to about $3.30 per share in 2027-2028 and recovers only to $3.60 by FY2031. The market keeps applying a discount, and the multiple compresses to 9x DE. The five-year target is $32.40, 16% below today, partly offset by about $7 of dividends. The balance sheet prevents a worse outcome: corporate net debt is small relative to investments and accrued carry, and the fixed dividend is covered more than twice by bear-case DE.

## 12. Base Case

The most likely outcome is a slow-building recovery. Q3 and Q4 2026 DE land between the weak consensus and the strong first half, with fewer transaction fees and lighter realizations than Q2. FY2026 DE comes in around $3.70 per share. In 2027 management fees begin to grow as the next U.S. buyout fund holds its first closes and AlpInvest flagships activate, and margin edges up as management guided. FRE grows about 8% a year to roughly $1.96 billion by FY2031.

Carry recovers gradually as exit markets reopen, contributing about $1 per share a year in realized net performance revenue and principal income. Buybacks keep the share count falling by 1-2% a year after equity grants. After-tax DE reaches $5.40 per share in FY2031, a 6.6% compound growth rate from the trailing $3.93. The market applies 11.5 times DE, slightly below the 11.8-12.9x forward multiple of 2024-2025 to reflect the industry's lower valuations. The five-year target is $62.10, 61% above today, or about 10% a year, plus a 3.6% dividend yield.

The base case is above the $52.21 average analyst target, but the analyst figure is a one-year target. The base case's implied one-year price of about $42-$43 is well below the analyst average, so the base case is not more aggressive than the street in the near term.

### Primary Framework

The primary lens is P/E on after-tax DE per share. DE is the earnings measure that drives the dividend, the buyback and the sell-side estimates, and it captures both recurring fees and realized carry. GAAP P/E is unusable because of mark-to-market carry, and FCF is unusable because consolidated CLO activity dominates the cash flow statement. EV-based multiples must use corporate EV, not the provider EV.

The cross-check is a sum of the parts. Using 15 times after-tax FRE ($41.85), after-tax net accrued carry ($5.35), balance sheet investments ($9.41) and subtracting corporate net debt ($3.91) gives $52.76 per share, almost exactly the $52.21 analyst average. A 15x multiple is a discount to the largest alternative managers and roughly in line with Carlyle's own history; the point is that a conservative multiple on fees alone, plus assets already on the balance sheet, is worth about 37% more than the current price.

### WACC

Using a 4.999% 10-year Treasury yield (CNBC, October 23, 2026), a 5% equity risk premium and StockAnalysis's five-year beta of 1.87, the CAPM cost of equity is 14.35%. With $2.65 billion of corporate debt at about 5.8% pre-tax and a 21% tax rate, WACC is 12.77%. StockAnalysis estimates 7.15%. The high beta reflects the stock's sensitivity to market sentiment about carry and private markets more than fundamental leverage. The model uses the WACC as context, not as a discount rate for the targets.

At the current price, the market implies roughly flat DE at a high cost of capital, and values the fee business near 10 times after-tax FRE once carry and investments are separated.

## 15. Risks And Red Flags

The biggest risk is that Carlyle's fee growth stalls while the market waits for the fundraising super cycle. Management fees fell 5% year over year in Q2, and the growth in FRE came from transaction fees and fee-related performance revenue that are more cyclical. If flagship funds raise less than expected or later than expected, the bull case loses its foundation and the multiple can stay compressed for years.

Credit risk is the second. Carlyle's Global Credit segment is its largest by AUM at $211.1 billion, and it includes direct lending, opportunistic credit, asset-backed finance and CLOs. A default cycle among AI-disrupted borrowers would hit fee-related performance revenue in credit vehicles, carry, and possibly CLO equity positions on Carlyle's balance sheet.

Carry risk is the third. Net accrued carry fell from $2.86 billion to $2.41 billion in six months and is concentrated in Corporate Private Equity. Lower marks in the seventh U.S. buyout fund were cited. If exit markets stay slow, realized carry falls short and DE disappoints even if FRE grows.

Other risks are smaller but real: equity compensation dilution that absorbs buybacks; the wealth channel's exposure to redemption pressure in a downturn; regulatory scrutiny of private credit and retail access to private markets; and the fact that Carlyle's high beta makes the stock fall hard in market selloffs regardless of fundamentals.

## 18. Conclusion

Carlyle at $38.59 trades at 9.8 times trailing distributable earnings and about 10 times after-tax fee-related earnings once its carry pool, investments and modest net debt are valued separately. That is a low price for a $485 billion private-markets platform with a 47% FRE margin, record fundraising in the first half of 2026 and a clean corporate balance sheet. The model's probability-weighted fair value is $58.97, 53% above the current price before dividends, with a bear case of $32.40 and a base case of $62.10; the sum-of-the-parts cross-check is $52.76.

The stance is **Watch / Positive**. The main reason to care is that the price assumes no growth while fundraising is accelerating and buybacks are accretive at this multiple. The main risk is that management fees keep shrinking and Q2's record transaction fees prove temporary, leaving Carlyle as a permanently discounted manager with lumpy carry.

The next trigger is the November 5 Q3 2026 report. It should be judged on segment management fees, DE against a deliberately low consensus, credit marks and any update on the next U.S. buyout fund, not on GAAP earnings.
