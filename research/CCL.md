## 1. Thesis In One Page

### The Setup

Carnival has completed the operational phase of its post-pandemic recovery and entered the harder financial phase: turning record-scale demand into durable per-share cash flow while paying down the debt accumulated when ships could not sail. The company now produces $27.31 billion of trailing revenue, $4.44 billion of operating income, $3.07 billion of net income and $3.20 billion of free cash flow. Those figures compare with $12.17 billion of revenue, a $3.95 billion operating loss and negative $6.61 billion of FCF in FY2022. The recovery is therefore real, measurable and already visible in cash rather than only adjusted earnings. (StockAnalysis financials and cash-flow pages, data through May 31, 2026, retrieved September 10, 2026.)

The opportunity is that equity valuation still resembles a challenged cyclical. At $22.70, CCL trades at 10.41 times trailing earnings, 9.72 times forward adjusted earnings and 9.72 times trailing FCF. Consensus calls for FY2026 revenue of $27.64 billion and adjusted EPS of $2.24, followed by headline FY2027 revenue of $28.62 billion and adjusted EPS of $2.63. The sell-side average target is $35.30, with a low of $28.70 and a high of $43.00. (StockAnalysis statistics and forecast pages, updated September 9-10, 2026.) The stock does not require a return to pandemic-recovery growth rates; it requires modest revenue growth, resilient pricing, and continued conversion of EBITDA into lower debt and higher EPS.

The risk is that CCL remains a leveraged owner of depreciating, fuel-consuming assets selling discretionary vacations. Net debt is 7.5 times trailing FCF, debt is 3.38 times EBITDA and interest coverage is only 3.67 times. A fuel shock, recession, geopolitical itinerary disruption, disease event or refinancing squeeze can therefore affect equity value much more than revenue. The September 9 quote also sits at the bottom of a $22.70-$34.03 52-week range after fuel prices became an immediate market concern. (StockAnalysis overview and statistics pages, retrieved September 10, 2026.) Cheap equity is partly compensation for a still-expensive capital structure.

The core thesis is that Carnival’s multi-brand fleet and controlled destination strategy can sustain low-single-digit revenue growth and mid-teens operating margins while FCF funds debt reduction. If that happens, declining interest expense can make EPS grow faster than revenue, allowing even an 11 times forward multiple on $3.00 of normalized earnings to support $33 per share. If fuel and consumer weakness hold EPS near $2.20 and the market applies 8 times, value falls to $17.60. The asymmetry is attractive but not yet decisive before September 28 earnings.

## 2. Company Story

Carnival grew from a single cruise operation into a portfolio spanning contemporary, premium and luxury brands. Its current brand system includes Carnival Cruise Line, Princess, Holland America, Cunard, AIDA, Costa, P&O Cruises in the UK and Australia, and Seabourn. It also owns and operates destination and tour assets, including ports, islands, hotels, lodges, railcars and motorcoaches. (StockAnalysis company profile, retrieved September 10, 2026.) The strategy is not one uniform cruise product; it is shared fleet, procurement, destination and financing capability behind brands aimed at different geographies and price points.

COVID-19 broke the model temporarily but transformed the balance sheet permanently. Revenue collapsed to $1.91 billion in FY2021 and net loss reached $9.50 billion. Carnival financed survival with debt and equity, taking total debt to $35.88 billion by FY2022 and expanding the share base. Sailing resumed, revenue recovered to $21.59 billion in FY2023 and $25.02 billion in FY2024, and net income returned to $1.92 billion in FY2024 before reaching $2.76 billion in FY2025. (StockAnalysis financials and balance-sheet pages, retrieved September 10, 2026.) The operational comeback is finished; repairing the financing legacy is not.

The recent story is disciplined normalization. Debt fell from $35.88 billion in FY2022 to $31.91 billion in FY2023, $28.88 billion in FY2024, $27.99 billion in FY2025 and $26.17 billion by May 2026. FCF improved from negative $6.61 billion in FY2022 to $997 million in FY2023, $1.30 billion in FY2024, $2.61 billion in FY2025 and $3.20 billion TTM. (StockAnalysis balance-sheet and cash-flow pages.) Carnival has moved from financing losses to choosing among repayment, dividends, fleet investment and repurchases.

The company matters because cruising is both hospitality and transportation wrapped into one controlled ecosystem. Carnival earns the ticket, food, beverage, casino, excursion, retail, spa, connectivity and destination spending around a captive multi-day guest. That raises revenue per passenger beyond the initial fare. The same integration raises fixed costs: ships must be crewed, maintained, insured and financed regardless of whether the last cabins clear at attractive prices.

## 3. Business Model

Passenger tickets generated $17.78 billion of trailing revenue and onboard and other activities generated $9.53 billion. The mix is approximately 65% ticket and 35% onboard/other. In FY2021, those figures were only $1.00 billion and $908 million; by FY2025 they had reached $17.42 billion and $9.20 billion. (StockAnalysis financial overview, retrieved September 10, 2026.) Onboard revenue is strategically important because each occupied cabin creates multiple selling opportunities after the ticket has already covered part of the voyage’s fixed cost.

The economic engine begins with capacity and load factor. A ship adds cabins only through fleet changes, but yield can rise through ticket price, itinerary, cabin mix and onboard spend. Carnival then manages variable costs—food, commissions, fuel, port fees—and large fixed or semi-fixed costs—crew, maintenance, dry docks, insurance, depreciation and interest. Incremental revenue above the fixed-cost base can carry strong margins, but the mechanism reverses when occupancy or pricing falls.

Customer deposits create favorable working-capital economics. Guests pay before sailing, financing operations in advance, but deposits are not free capital: Carnival owes a voyage or refund. Current liabilities were $13.43 billion at May 2026, and standardized current unearned revenue has moved into broader “other current liabilities” presentation in recent periods. (StockAnalysis balance-sheet page.) The 0.33 current ratio therefore looks alarming under industrial-company rules but partly reflects prepaid bookings rather than conventional payable stress. Liquidity must still be judged against refunds, maturities and operating cash needs.

Unit economics should be assessed per available lower berth day and passenger cruise day: net revenue yield, occupancy, ticket yield, onboard revenue, cruise cost excluding fuel, fuel consumption and EBITDA per capacity day. The public StockAnalysis pages do not expose those operational KPIs, so this report does not invent them. The latest investor materials should be used to reconcile revenue growth into capacity, occupancy and price before capital is committed.

Carnival’s fleet is both moat and burden. A modern ship costs billions, takes years to design and build, and requires regulatory, port, crew and distribution capability. That limits entry at global scale. Yet ships depreciate, consume fuel and require recurring dry docks and upgrades. TTM depreciation and amortization was $2.86 billion and capex was $3.59 billion. (StockAnalysis cash-flow page.) Any valuation that adds back D&A without acknowledging replacement economics is too generous.

## 4. Products, Segments, And Moat

The portfolio covers mass-market through luxury cruising and multiple source markets. Carnival Cruise Line provides scale and U.S. contemporary exposure; Princess and Holland America address premium demand; Cunard and Seabourn add luxury and heritage positioning; AIDA, Costa and P&O create European and Australian distribution. This segmentation lets Carnival price discriminate and reuse corporate capabilities without forcing every guest into one proposition.

| Exposure | Economic Role | Advantage | Principal Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| North America contemporary | Largest-volume demand pool | Scale, awareness and broad departure access | U.S. consumer and fuel sensitivity |
| Premium brands | Higher ticket and onboard potential | Loyalty, itineraries and service differentiation | Older demographics and brand capex |
| Europe operations | Geographic diversification | Local brands and home-port networks | FX, regulation and weaker growth |
| Luxury / expedition | High spend per guest | Scarcity and service premium | Small scale and geopolitical itinerary risk |
| Owned destinations | Capture more guest wallet | Controlled experience and differentiated itineraries | Upfront capital and weather concentration |

Carnival’s moat is moderate, not absolute. Scale supports procurement, advertising, financing, revenue management and destination development. Brand familiarity and loyalty reduce acquisition cost. Port slots, itineraries and ship supply constrain competitors. But customers can compare Royal Caribbean, Norwegian, land resorts and independent travel with little switching cost. The moat is strongest in cost and asset scale, weaker in exclusive customer ownership.

Controlled destinations may deepen the moat by moving more of the vacation inside Carnival’s ecosystem. A destination can increase perceived itinerary value and capture food, beverage, retail and excursion spend while giving the company more control over guest experience. The risk is capital allocation: destination projects can become expensive fixed assets exposed to weather, utilization and changing traveler preferences. Returns should be evaluated using incremental guest spend and itinerary pricing, not promotional attendance statistics.

Relative positioning matters. Royal Caribbean is widely perceived as stronger in large-ship attractions and private-destination economics, while Norwegian competes through brand and itinerary variety. Carnival’s advantage is unmatched portfolio breadth and scale; its disadvantage is the largest pandemic debt legacy. A sustained multiple rerating therefore requires balance-sheet convergence as much as operational convergence.

## 6. Quality Of Earnings

Carnival’s current earnings quality is **clean but capital-intensive**. Revenue, operating income, net income and cash flow all point in the same direction. OCF exceeds net income for understandable reasons—principally D&A on ships and positive booking working capital—rather than because receivables are expanding or unusual gains dominate the P&L. TTM net income of $3.07 billion converted to $6.79 billion of OCF and $3.20 billion of FCF after capex. (StockAnalysis financials and cash-flow pages.)

The main adjustment is economic depreciation. Ships are long-lived assets, but they require maintenance, dry docks, environmental upgrades and eventual replacement. D&A of $2.86 billion and capex of $3.59 billion are close enough that D&A is not a meaningless non-cash charge. FCF correctly captures current cash investment, but any one-year capex figure can be distorted by newbuild delivery timing. Normalized owner earnings should use a multi-year maintenance and committed-newbuild schedule rather than assume all current capex is permanent or optional.

Working capital deserves a second adjustment. Customer deposits are positive financing in ordinary conditions, and rising bookings can lift OCF before voyages are delivered. That is economically valuable, but it can reverse during cancellations or shutdowns. TTM change in working capital was only $132 million, far below the $1.53 billion FY2023 inflow, so current OCF is not heavily dependent on another reopening-era deposit surge. (StockAnalysis cash-flow page.) This improves confidence in the current FCF base.

Tax is unusually favorable. The TTM effective tax rate is 0.71% and cash tax is $22 million. (StockAnalysis statistics page.) Cruise operators benefit from maritime and tonnage-tax regimes, but an investor should not automatically capitalize a sub-1% rate forever. Changes in U.S. policy, global minimum tax rules, source-market mix or onshore businesses could raise the burden. The workbook uses a 5% normalized tax rate for WACC rather than pretending the reported rate is a conventional corporate tax rate.

Adjusted forecast EPS is not identical to GAAP EPS. StockAnalysis states that FY2026 and FY2027 forecast EPS and forward P/E are based on non-GAAP adjusted numbers. FY2025’s displayed adjusted EPS is $2.25 while reported EPS on the financial overview is $2.02. (StockAnalysis forecast and financials pages.) Valuation should compare like with like and maintain a bridge for fuel derivatives, debt extinguishment, ship disposals and restructuring items.

SBC is not a major distortion at $103 million, only 3.2% of FCF, but share count still rose 1.09% year over year despite $381 million of TTM repurchases. (StockAnalysis cash-flow and statistics pages.) The likely bridge includes dual-listed share mechanics, option exercises and other issuance. Buybacks create value only if they reduce diluted shares after all issuance.

## 7. Forward Projections And Scenario Model

### Analyst Expectations And Guidance

Public consensus expects FY2026 revenue of $27.64 billion, up 3.81%, operating income of $4.29 billion, net income of $3.05 billion, adjusted EPS of $2.24 and FCF of $3.67 billion. Twenty-five analysts contribute to the visible FY2026 estimate. StockAnalysis’s headline also shows FY2027 revenue of $28.62 billion, up 3.55%, and adjusted EPS of $2.63, up 17.43%. (StockAnalysis forecast page, updated September 9, 2026.) Later detailed rows are gated, so this report does not present hidden FY2027/FY2028 data as verified consensus.

The important shape is slow revenue and faster EPS. Consensus effectively assumes that margin, interest or share count provides leverage: a 3.55% revenue increase accompanies 17.43% adjusted EPS growth. That can happen if debt repayment lowers interest and fleet/destination mix improves profit per guest. It fails if fuel, labor and refinancing costs absorb the benefit.

### Estimate Revision Trend

The visible FY2026 adjusted EPS estimate is $2.24 versus $2.25 in FY2025, a 0.36% decline, while FY2027 rebounds to $2.63. This is a pause-and-reacceleration pattern rather than a straight-line recovery. The forecast page shows 18 Strong Buy, five Buy and seven Hold ratings in August, with no Sell ratings; the analyst count increased to 30 from 28 in March. (StockAnalysis forecast page.) That breadth is constructive but also means a positive consensus is already well known.

Recent individual targets remain above the stock: Citi maintained Buy and $37 on September 9; Morgan Stanley reiterated Buy and $31 on September 4; Bernstein maintained Hold and $29 on August 28; Truist maintained Hold and $31 on July 29; Bank of America reiterated Buy while reducing its target from $45 to $42 on July 15. (StockAnalysis forecast page.) The mix says analysts see upside but disagree on how much balance-sheet and fuel risk deserve to suppress the multiple.

### Forecast Logic

The bear case does not assume another shutdown. It assumes revenue barely grows after FY2027, fuel and labor prevent margin expansion, and normalized adjusted EPS settles at $2.20. An 8 times multiple produces $17.60, 22.5% downside. This is a genuine bear target below current price and reflects cyclical compression without requiring insolvency.

The base case compounds revenue 3.5% after FY2027, broadly matching the visible three-year 3.9% forecast growth rate. Debt reduction and stable margins lift terminal adjusted EPS to $3.00. At 11 times forward earnings, fair value is $33.00, 45.4% above $22.70 and within 6.5% of the $35.30 analyst average target.

The bull case assumes 5.5% revenue growth, durable pricing, stronger owned-destination economics and faster interest savings. Terminal adjusted EPS reaches $3.50 and a 13 times multiple produces $45.50. This is slightly above the current $43 analyst high and should therefore be treated as an upside case, not the central estimate.

### Reverse Valuation

At $22.70 and 9.72 times forward P/E, the market implies approximately $2.34 of forward adjusted EPS, close to the midpoint between FY2026’s $2.24 and FY2027’s $2.63. If investors eventually pay 11 times, today’s price requires only $2.06 of sustainable earnings. Conversely, at $2.63 of FY2027 consensus EPS, today’s price is 8.63 times. (StockAnalysis overview and forecast pages; author calculations.) The equity price therefore embeds skepticism, not growth exuberance.

The enterprise-value view is less cheap. EV/EBITDA is 7.53 times and EV/FCF is 17.19 times versus P/FCF of 9.72 times. (StockAnalysis statistics page.) This difference is the balance sheet. Equity holders receive the benefit of deleveraging, but they also bear amplified loss if enterprise value falls. Forward P/E is primary because net debt is 7.5 times FCF; FCF-exit equity valuation in this gray zone can generate implausibly wide targets from small operating changes.

### Projection Takeaway

The model requires execution, not heroic top-line growth. Base revenue growth is below historical recovery rates and the exit multiple is only modestly above today’s forward multiple. Most upside comes from proving that $3.00 of normalized adjusted EPS is achievable as interest falls. The key falsifier is a quarter in which yield and bookings remain healthy but fuel and other costs prevent EPS and debt improvement.

## 8. Management, Governance, And Capital Allocation

Management’s post-pandemic record is best judged by debt reduction and cash conversion. Lowering debt from $35.88 billion in FY2022 to $26.17 billion by May 2026 while restoring profitability is a material achievement. Net debt repayment was $4.90 billion in FY2023, $2.34 billion in FY2024, $1.78 billion in FY2025 and $2.05 billion TTM. (StockAnalysis cash-flow page.) The pace is uneven because maturities are refinanced, but the direction is clear.

Capital allocation has become more balanced. Carnival spent $3.59 billion on capex, paid $414 million of dividends, repurchased $381 million of shares and still reduced net debt by $2.05 billion TTM. (StockAnalysis cash-flow page.) The dividend’s 20.65% payout ratio and 2.64% yield are conservative relative to earnings. (StockAnalysis statistics page.) However, every dollar returned while debt costs roughly 4.6% pre-tax must be justified against accelerated repayment and fleet investment.

Repurchases are less persuasive than debt reduction until diluted shares consistently fall. Current shares rose 1.09% year over year, while the TTM filing-date share count is 1.37 billion and total common shares are 1.372 billion. (StockAnalysis statistics and balance-sheet pages.) Management should disclose whether repurchases merely offset SBC, convertible or dual-listed issuance.

The dual-listed corporate structure and maritime tax framework add governance complexity. Economic exposure spans Carnival Corporation and Carnival plc, multiple jurisdictions and regulated ship-owning entities. This report does not claim a conclusion on executive incentives, related-party matters or board independence without the latest proxy. The near-term governance test is simpler: does management prioritize net leverage and per-share cash earnings over capacity growth for its own sake?

## 9. Market Debate And Variant Perception

### What Bulls Believe

Bulls believe cruising retains a value advantage versus comparable land vacations and that Carnival can raise ticket price without losing occupancy. Onboard revenue provides a second monetization layer, controlled destinations deepen wallet capture, and new ships improve fuel efficiency and guest economics. With P/FCF below 10 times, even modest growth plus debt reduction can create double-digit equity returns.

They also believe the balance-sheet story is underappreciated. Each $1 billion of debt repayment reduces risk, can lower interest and moves enterprise value toward common equity. Debt has already fallen $9.71 billion from FY2022. If TTM FCF of $3.20 billion grows toward FY2026 consensus $3.67 billion, Carnival can continue paying down debt without abandoning fleet quality. (StockAnalysis balance-sheet, cash-flow and forecast pages.)

### What Bears Believe

Bears believe the stock is cheap because the business is structurally volatile. Fuel costs can rise before ticket pricing resets. Consumer stress can hit close-in bookings and onboard spend. Geopolitical events can reroute ships. Environmental rules can force retrofit or fuel expense. An outbreak can damage the entire category. The company’s beta of 2.31 and 28.1% 52-week decline demonstrate the equity’s sensitivity. (StockAnalysis statistics page.)

They also focus on the enterprise denominator. $31.09 billion of common value supports $55.02 billion of enterprise value. A 10% fall in EV, all else equal, would remove $5.50 billion—17.7% of market capitalization. This leverage makes “only” moderate operating disappointments material for shareholders. FCF can also fall quickly when newbuild deliveries cluster.

### What The Market May Be Missing

The positive variant is that the market treats debt as static even while FCF actively retires it. If Carnival directs $2-$3 billion annually to net repayment, net debt can fall into the high teens over several years. The multiple need not expand much: interest savings and lower risk can raise EPS and valuation simultaneously.

The negative variant is that current margin represents favorable recovery pricing and deferred vacation demand rather than a through-cycle base. Operating margin has already slipped from 16.37% in FY2025 to 16.25% TTM despite revenue growth. (StockAnalysis financials page.) A small decline is not a trend, but it says incremental revenue no longer guarantees automatic margin expansion.

Fuel is the immediate test. News around the September selloff emphasized that Carnival spends more than $1 billion annually on diesel. (TipRanks headline linked from StockAnalysis overview, September 2026.) Without a verified hedge schedule, the magnitude remains uncertain. September 28 earnings should reveal whether fuel is a manageable bridge or a reset to consensus.

## 10. Bull Case

The bull case requires durable demand, not another reopening wave. Ticket yield rises with only modest capacity growth, onboard spend per guest continues to increase, and controlled destinations support differentiated pricing. Revenue compounds 5.5% from the FY2027 anchor while gross margin remains near or above 55% and operating margin expands modestly.

FCF remains above $3.5 billion through the cycle, allowing rapid debt retirement after maintenance and committed fleet capex. Lower debt reduces annual interest and refinancing risk. Adjusted EPS reaches $3.50 without aggressive share-count assumptions. A 13 times forward multiple—still moderate for a company with cleaner leverage and mid-single-digit growth—supports $45.50.

Evidence would include net yield growth above capacity growth, stable occupancy without heavy discounting, cruise cost excluding fuel under control, net debt/EBITDA moving below 3 times and diluted shares declining. The bull case breaks if destination investment or newbuild capex absorbs the FCF expected to repay debt.

## 11. Bear Case

The bear case assumes a normal cyclical disappointment rather than catastrophe. Consumers trade down, close-in pricing softens and onboard spend slows. Fuel and labor rise faster than net yield, causing operating margin to fall even while revenue remains approximately flat. Debt still declines, but more slowly, and refinancing offsets interest savings.

Adjusted EPS settles at $2.20, close to current TTM earnings rather than collapsing. The market pays 8 times because debt remains above 3 times EBITDA and the next upcycle is unclear. The resulting $17.60 target is 22.5% below current price. This downside is meaningful but not existential; it is the correct counterweight to a base case with 45% upside.

The early signals would be lower booking pace, increased promotions, falling onboard spend per passenger, occupancy maintained by price cuts, weaker FCF guidance and debt/EBITDA stalling. A dividend increase while leverage stalls would worsen the case by suggesting capital-allocation drift.

## 12. Base Case

The base case assumes cruising remains healthy but normalizes. FY2027 revenue reaches the public $28.62 billion headline estimate, then compounds 3.5%. Price and onboard spend carry more of growth than capacity. Operating margin stays in the mid-teens as fuel and labor offset mix and scale efficiencies.

FCF supports both maintenance/newbuild commitments and continued debt reduction. Interest savings let adjusted EPS outgrow revenue and reach $3.00. The share count is broadly flat because repurchases offset issuance. At 11 times, fair value is $33.00. This does not require the market to award a premium multiple; it requires Carnival to become a less leveraged version of the business already visible.

The base case remains conditional on September 28. Investors need a clear bridge from bookings and yields to fuel-adjusted EBITDA, a debt-repayment plan and evidence that FY2027 EPS growth is not merely a stale consensus artifact.

Forward P/E is the least misleading primary lens because earnings incorporate depreciation, interest and the common-share claim. EV/EBITDA is useful for peer and capital-structure comparison, while FCF yield tests actual cash. A pure FCF exit model is not primary because net debt equals 7.5 times FCF; small changes in enterprise assumptions produce disproportionately large common-equity swings.

The model’s $33 base target compares with analyst targets of $28.70 low, $35.30 average and $43 high. It is 6.5% below the average and therefore calibrated rather than contrarian for its own sake. The $45.50 bull case modestly exceeds the sell-side high, while the $17.60 bear case is well below the range because analyst targets are not designed as recession scenarios.

The probability-weighted $32.27 value implies 42.2% price upside before dividends. That sounds large but should be discounted for event timing: the market has immediate information on September 28. The expected value is not a forecast that shares move directly to $32; it is a framework for whether current compensation is adequate for leverage and volatility.

## 14. Catalysts

The first catalyst is September 28, 2026 earnings before market open. The report should update net yields, occupancy, booking curve, onboard spend, fuel price/consumption, cruise cost excluding fuel, adjusted EBITDA, FCF and net debt. The most valuable disclosure would be a quantified FY2027 bridge showing how much EPS growth comes from operations versus lower interest.

The second catalyst is continued debt retirement. Each quarterly balance sheet can demonstrate whether net debt/EBITDA is moving toward 3 times. Credit-rating upgrades or refinancing at lower spreads would convert operating recovery into a lower cost of capital.

The third catalyst is proof from controlled destinations and loyalty initiatives. Carnival Rewards and associated card economics may increase engagement and data, while destination investment can lift wallet capture. These initiatives matter only if they raise net yield and return on invested capital after marketing and construction cost.

The fourth catalyst is fuel normalization. A decline in bunker and diesel prices or better hedging can remove a near-term earnings overhang. Conversely, sustained higher fuel before pricing catches up is a negative catalyst.

## 15. Risks And Red Flags

The largest risk is leverage interacting with cyclicality. Carnival does not need another pandemic for equity to fall materially; a moderate reduction in enterprise value can have a much larger percentage effect on common value. Debt maturity and refinancing terms therefore matter alongside bookings.

| Risk | Mechanism | Evidence To Monitor |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer slowdown | Lower fares, occupancy or onboard spend | Booking curve, close-in pricing, cancellation rate |
| Fuel inflation | Cost rises before ticket repricing | Fuel per ton, consumption and hedges |
| Refinancing | Higher coupons offset principal reduction | Maturity ladder and cash interest |
| Fleet capex | Newbuild/retrofit spending suppresses FCF | Five-year capex commitments |
| Regulation | Emissions, port or labor rules raise cost | Compliance capex and itinerary changes |
| Geopolitics/weather | Rerouting reduces yield and raises fuel | Cancelled ports and compensation |
| Health event | Category-wide demand/refund shock | Deposits, liquidity and cancellations |
| Dilution | Buybacks fail to reduce share count | Diluted shares and issuance bridge |

The Altman Z-score is 1.31, below the conventional 3 threshold, while the Piotroski F-score is 7. (StockAnalysis statistics page.) The Z-score is not a prediction of failure and can be poorly calibrated for asset-heavy cruise operators with customer deposits, but it reinforces that the balance sheet cannot be treated as repaired.

The current ratio is 0.33 and working capital is negative $8.94 billion. (StockAnalysis statistics page.) Customer deposits explain part of this structure, but a refund shock can turn favorable working capital into a cash demand. Liquidity analysis should therefore include available revolvers and deposit behavior, not only cash.

## 16. What Would Change My Mind

I would become more bullish if net debt/EBITDA fell below 3 times while FCF remained above $3.5 billion, diluted shares declined, and management demonstrated net yield growth after fuel. A transparent FY2027 bridge supporting at least $2.60 adjusted EPS without aggressive add-backs would strengthen confidence that interest savings and operations are both contributing.

I would become more bearish if operating margin fell below 14%, FY2027 adjusted EPS expectations moved below $2.30, net debt stopped declining for two quarters, or management raised capital returns despite weaker FCF. Evidence that occupancy is being maintained through discounting, or that destination/newbuild capex structurally keeps FCF below $2 billion, would invalidate the base case.

## 18. Conclusion

Carnival at $22.70 offers a real cash-recovery thesis at a low earnings multiple. TTM FCF of $3.20 billion, debt reduction of $9.71 billion since FY2022 and FY2027 adjusted EPS of $2.63 support the idea that per-share earnings can grow faster than low-single-digit revenue. The base case of $33 and weighted value of $32.27 do not require a premium valuation.

The reason to remain at Watch is the $23.93 billion net-debt claim and an immediate earnings event. At 7.5 times FCF, debt amplifies both improvement and disappointment. Fuel, consumer demand and capex can all interrupt the clean recovery line. The main reason to own is deleveraging-driven EPS growth; the main risk is that cost or demand weakens before the balance sheet reaches a safer level. September 28 earnings is the next trigger.
